# Definition
> [!info] Shell Theorem[^1]
> A uniform, thin spherical shell of mass attracts an external mass gravitationally exactly as though the shell's entire mass were concentrated at its center; a mass located anywhere inside the shell experiences zero net gravitational force from it.

# Properties
- Outside the shell, the [[Gravitational Potential Energy|potential energy]] of an external mass depends only on the shell's total mass and its distance to the shell's center, not on how the mass is distributed over the shell's surface.
- Inside the shell, the potential energy is the same at every point, independent of position, so no net force acts anywhere in the interior; the pulls from nearer and farther portions of the shell cancel exactly.
- A solid sphere (e.g. a planet) can be decomposed into concentric shells, so the theorem extends to it: a sphere with spherically symmetric mass distribution attracts external masses exactly as if all of its mass were concentrated at its center, justifying the treatment of planets as point masses in [[Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation]].
- Depends on the exact inverse-square dependence of [[Gravitation|gravitational]] force on distance: nearer portions of the shell pull more strongly per unit mass but subtend a smaller solid angle as seen from the external point, while farther portions pull more weakly but subtend a larger one, and the two effects cancel exactly.

[^1]: [The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Vol. I, Ch. 13: Work and Potential Energy (A)](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_13.html)
