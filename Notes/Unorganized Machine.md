# Definition
> [!info] Unorganized Machine[^1]
> A machine whose structure is built up in a largely random way from standard components (e.g. a random arrangement of interconnected neuron-like units), as opposed to one designed for a definite purpose, and which can subsequently be organised into a useful machine by suitable interfering training.

# Types
- A-type unorganized machine - about the simplest model of a [[Nervous System|nervous system]] with a random arrangement of [[Neuron|neurons]].[^1]
- B-type unorganized machine - an A-type machine whose connections pass through modifiers that can be switched on or off by external interference, making the machine trainable.
- P-type unorganized machine - an unorganized machine trained by reward and punishment inputs; see [[Pleasure-Pain System]].

# Properties
- Turing restricts attention to discrete controlling machinery: brains very nearly fall into this class and could likely be made genuinely discrete without changing their essential properties. Discreteness is only an advantage to the theorist and serves no evolutionary purpose, so Nature cannot be expected to produce truly discrete brains (cf. [[Argument from Continuity in the Nervous System]]).[^2]
- **Infant cortex as unorganized machine**: many parts of the brain are definite circuits for definite purposes (reflexes proper, elementary analysis of shapes and sounds), but intellectual activities are too varied for this. Large parts of the [[Cerebral Cortex|cortex]] have largely indeterminate function; in the infant their effect is uncoordinated, in the adult it is purposive and depends on training in childhood, with a large remnant of infantile random behaviour remaining. Differences such as which language one speaks are due to different training, not different brain development.[^3]
- Hence the infant cortex is plausibly an unorganized machine that is organised by interfering training, possibly into a [[Universal Turing Machine|universal machine]] or something like it, able to obey orders in an appropriate language. Understanding the step from unorganized infant to universal machine is a prerequisite for understanding how the organising process yields a more normal type of mind.[^3]
- **Evolutionary cheapness**: very few genes would be needed to produce an A- or B-type machine, far fewer than for e.g. the respiratory centre. Yet an unorganized cortex is virtually useless without an attempt to organise it: a mutation granting one would confer little selective advantage unless it arose in a milieu with speech in which information can be passed between generations (cf. cultural search in [[Intellectual Search]]).[^4]
- **Positive argument for thinking machinery**: machinery can already imitate any small part of a person (microphone for the ear, camera for the eye). Copying nerves electrically in detail would be pointless, like building cars that walk on legs instead of using wheels; electronic circuits already have the essential properties of nerves, namely transmitting and storing information. Nerves are more compact, durable, and energy-efficient, but circuits are so much faster that this may outweigh those advantages.[^5]
- A precursor of connectionism: B-type machines are randomly connected networks of neuron-like units trained by modifying connections, anticipating the [[Perceptron]] and artificial neural networks.
- The machine to be educated in the [[Child Machine]] proposal.
