# Definition
> [!info] Four Horsemen (Gottman)[^1]
> Four patterns of conflict that predict relationship breakdown in John Gottman's observational research:
> 1. **Criticism**: attacking the partner's character ("You never think of anyone but yourself"), as opposed to a *complaint* about a specific behavior.
> 2. **Contempt**: mockery, sarcasm and disgust. This is the strongest predictor.
> 3. **Defensiveness**: counter-attacks and excuses.
> 4. **Stonewalling**: withdrawing from the interaction.

# Properties
- **Evidence.**
	- In longitudinal studies, these behaviors during conflict discussions predicted later divorce (Gottman and Levenson, 2000).
	- Stable couples showed about 5 positive interactions for every negative one during conflict (Gottman, 1994).
	- The famous "over 90% accuracy" prediction figures came from fitting models to the same data they predicted, so they are inflated (Heyman, 2001). The direction of the findings is well supported.
- **How to raise a problem: the softened startup.** Describe the *situation* and your *feeling*, then make a positive request. "I feel overwhelmed when the dishes pile up; could you do them tonight?" instead of "You're so lazy." How a conflict discussion starts predicts how it ends (Gottman et al., 1998). This answers the question Carnegie leaves open, how to get a partner to change without criticism: be specific, focus on behavior, and make a request rather than a verdict.[^2]
- **Appreciation.** Carnegie cites a study claiming "lack of appreciation" was the main reason wives ran away.[^3] That study cannot be traced. Gottman's research independently finds that a habit of expressing fondness and admiration protects relationships, and expressed gratitude predicts relationship maintenance (Gordon et al., 2012).
- **Repair.** Admitting your part ("If you are wrong, admit it quickly") is a *repair attempt*, one of the strongest buffers against escalation.[^4]
