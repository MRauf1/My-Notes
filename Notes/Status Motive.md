# Definition
> [!info] Status Motive[^1]
> The desire for respect, admiration and voluntary deference from others. It is one of the best-supported candidates for a fundamental human motive (Anderson, Hildreth and Howland, 2015). Carnegie, following Dewey ("the desire to be important") and William James ("the craving to be appreciated"), makes it the master key of human relations.

# Properties
- **Evidence.** Anderson, Hildreth and Howland (2015) reviewed the evidence on status. People in every studied culture care about it. Gaining or losing status changes well-being and health. People work hard to gain it and to defend it. The desire to *belong*, to have stable, caring relationships, is an equally fundamental need (Baumeister and Leary, 1995).
- **Not unique to humans.** Carnegie claims that the desire for importance distinguishes humans from animals.[^2] This is false. Dominance and status hierarchies are widespread in social animals, from chickens' pecking orders to chimpanzees. In primates, rank affects stress hormones and health (Sapolsky, 2005). What *is* more distinctive in humans is prestige: status freely given for skill or knowledge, not taken by force (Henrich and Gil-White, 2001).
- **Not the only motive.**
	- Freud's claim that everything springs from "the sex urge and the desire to be great" is not supported.[^2]
	- Modern frameworks list several basic needs. Self-determination theory proposes autonomy, competence and relatedness (Deci and Ryan, 2000).
	- Fundamental-motives research also lists self-protection, affiliation, status, mate acquisition and retention, and kin care (Kenrick et al., 2010).
- **Practical consequence.** Many of Carnegie's techniques work by giving the other person status rather than taking it: honest appreciation, letting them save face, letting them excel ("if you want friends, let your friends excel you").[^3] Unearned flattery fails because people detect insincerity, though flattery is often more effective than people expect (Chan and Sengupta, 2010).[^4]
