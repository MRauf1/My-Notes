# Definition
> [!info] Efficiency Trap (Burkeman)[^1]
> Moving faster through an effectively infinite incoming supply of something (information, email, tasks) never gets you to the end of it. You process more, faster, without ever the satisfaction of reducing it, and are left more scattered and stressed.

# Properties
- **Information.** Clay Shirky blamed "filter failure" rather than information overload. But the filters improved and the result was not greater calm. The real challenge is "haystack-sized piles of needles" (Nicholas Carr): there is too much that is genuinely valuable, not too much junk.[^1]
- **Three rules for infinite information**:[^2]
	1. Treat your to-read pile like a *river, not a bucket*: let most items float by and pick a few without guilt.
	2. *Don't stockpile knowledge.* Most of reading's long-term benefit comes from how it shapes your sensibility, not from stored facts ("Every book makes a mark, even if it doesn't stay in your conscious memory"). Elaborate note-taking systems can turn reading into a chore you then avoid.
	3. *Consuming information is a present-moment activity.* Sometimes read what is fun, for the sake of that half hour of being alive.
- "The art of being wise is the art of knowing what to overlook." (William James)[^3]
- **News and causes.** No one can function while feeling every injustice in the world as if it had happened to a loved one. Media and activist groups are locked in an arms race for attention and exaggerate everything. Some people end up "living inside the news". Ten minutes a day is enough to stay informed.[^4]
- **Pick your battles.** Embracing limits lets you do more for the causes you choose. In an age of attention scarcity, good citizenship may mean withdrawing attention from everything except the battles you have chosen.[^5]
- Related: [[Attention]], [[Imperfectionism]], [[Paradox of Limitation]].

- **Historical evidence.** Keynes predicted in 1930 that by now no one would need to work more than about 15 hours a week. Instead, rising wealth produced new needs and busyness became a status symbol.[^6] "Labour-saving" washing machines and vacuum cleaners saved housewives no time, because standards of cleanliness rose to match (Ruth Schwartz Cowan, *More Work for Mother*).[^7] The economic analogue is the *Jevons paradox*, or rebound effect: efficiency gains can increase total consumption.
