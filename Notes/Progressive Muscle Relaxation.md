# Definition
> [!info] Progressive Muscle Relaxation (PMR)[^1]
> Edmund Jacobson's (1938) technique: systematically tense and then release muscle groups to learn to recognize and reduce tension. Carnegie's summary: "You always begin to relax with your muscles," since "the mind and nerves can't relax while the muscles are tense."

# Properties
- **Evidence.**
	- Meta-analyses find relaxation training moderately reduces anxiety, $g \approx 0.57$ (Manzoni et al., 2008). Applied relaxation is an effective treatment for [[Generalized Anxiety Disorder|GAD]], roughly comparable to CBT in some trials (Öst and Breitholtz, 2000).
	- PMR improves sleep quality and is one component of relaxation training in [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia]].
- **What's wrong in Carnegie's account.**
	- The claim that the eyes "burn up one-fourth of all the nervous energies consumed by the body" is unfounded. The *visual cortex* is metabolically demanding, but eye muscles are not.[^2]
	- The cue-word method ("Let go… let go") is closer to autogenic training or self-suggestion. It is plausible as a relaxation cue, but evidence for it alone is thinner.[^3]
- **Effort paradox.** "Don't make an effort to relax. Relaxation is the absence of all tension and effort." Trying hard to relax or fall asleep can backfire. This is captured in sleep-effort research (Espie et al., 2006) and Wegner's ironic process theory.[^3]
- **Tension as habit.** People recruit unnecessary muscles during mental work ("we scowl when we concentrate"), believing effort must feel effortful. Learning to notice and release this tension is the core skill of PMR.[^4]
- Related: [[Mental Fatigue]], [[Stress]], [[Meditation]].
