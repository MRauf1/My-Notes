# Definition
> [!info] Dichotomy of Control[^1]
> ([[Epictetus]]) Some things are up to us and some are not. Up to us are judgment, inclination, desire, and aversion (in short, whatever is our own doing, i.e. our [[Prohairesis|will]] and the right use of [[Impression (Stoicism)|impressions]]). Not up to us are our bodies, possessions, reputations, public offices, family, and country (whatever is not our own doing).

# Properties
- What is up to us is naturally free, unimpeded, and unobstructed; what is not is weak, enslaved, subject to impediment, and not ours.[^1]
- Mistaking what is not yours for yours makes one obstructed, dejected, troubled, and blaming gods and men; regarding as yours only what is yours means no one can constrain or harm you, and you have no enemies.[^2]
- Good and bad lie only in what is up to us; everything else is [[Indifferent (Stoicism)|indifferent]]. Hence "where should we look for what is good? In will. And for what is bad? In will."[^3]
- The only resources needed for any circumstance are "the knowledge of what is and isn't mine, and of what is and isn't possible for me".[^4]
- To be free one must neither want nor try to avoid anything up to others; confine efforts to what is in your power.[^5]
- Invincibility: never enter a contest where victory isn't up to you.[^6]
- Paradox resolved: act with **confidence** toward what is not subject to will (it cannot harm you) and **caution** toward what is (where real harm is possible).[^7]
- Externals should nonetheless be treated with care, since the *use* we make of them is not indifferent, but with self-possession, since the things themselves are.[^8]
- Anxiety arises only from wanting what isn't up to us; no one is anxious about assenting to falsehoods.[^9]
- Under [[Stoic Determinism|determinism]], events are fated but our responses to them are not; thus the dichotomy locates responsibility wholly in inner life.[^10]
- Foundation of [[Freedom (Stoicism)]], the [[Three Disciplines (Epictetus)]], and [[Stoic Exercises]]; compare [[Cognitive Therapy]].

# Modern Echoes
- **Serenity Prayer.** "God grant me the serenity to accept the things I cannot change; the courage to change the things I can; and the wisdom to know the difference." Carnegie: "Co-operate with the inevitable."[^11] He quotes Epictetus: "There is only one way to happiness, and that is to cease worrying about things which are beyond the power of our will."[^12]
- **Evidence.** Acceptance-based therapies (Acceptance and Commitment Therapy) have meta-analytic support across anxiety, depression and chronic pain (A-Tjak et al., 2015). Acceptance must be paired with action on what *can* be changed: "If there be one, try to find it; if there be none, never mind it."[^13] Accepting the controllable leads to [[Learned Helplessness]] rather than serenity. See also [[Locus of Control]] and [[Decatastrophizing]].
- **Adlerian echo.** The [[Separation of Tasks]] applies the same split to relationships (others' judgments are their task), and [[Self-Acceptance]] cites the Serenity Prayer.
# Robertson
- Epictetus defined Stoic education itself as learning "what things are our own, and what are not" (*Discourses* 4.5.7). The distinction grounds two principles: continual self-awareness of one's judgements ([[Prosoche]]), and philosophical acceptance of the rest: "To make the best of what is in our power, and take the rest as it naturally happens" (*Discourses* 1.1.17).[^rob1]
- Zeno's doctrine "follow nature and make good use of impressions" amounts to serenely accepting what is outside our control, and questioning whether each impression concerns what is within it ([[Living in Accord with Nature]]).[^rob2]
- Emotional disturbance results from mindless absorption in externals: over-attachment to pleasure, wealth and praise, and over-anxiety about pain, poverty and criticism.[^rob3]
- **Economy of effort (Baudouin).** *Sustine et abstine*. Wishing for the impossible is running "our heads against a wall". Regret over the unchangeable past and excessive concern with the uncertain future waste energy; guilt is useful only as motivation to change today. The true locus of control is the here and now.[^rob4]
- **Descartes' third maxim**: "there is nothing that is completely within our power except our thoughts". Long practice makes this viewpoint habitual; Descartes is credited as an astute reader of Epictetus ([[Amor Fati]]).[^rob5]
