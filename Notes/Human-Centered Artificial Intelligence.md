# Definition
> [!info] Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence[^1]
> The position that the development and application of [[Artificial Intelligence|AI]], understood as the broad spectrum of computer tools designed to perform increasingly complex cognitive tasks, must remain fundamentally human-centred. Further development should prioritise benefit for humanity as a whole, AI applications should remain directly useful to humans (individually or collectively), and implementations should preserve and value the humanity of humans above their commodification.

# Properties
- The case *for* developing AI is twofold: it can radically augment natural human abilities, and it can expand what is possible beyond what humans can do individually or collectively.[^2]
- Principles:[^3]
	- For each use-case, articulate who the intended beneficiaries are.
	- Quantify human costs at local and global levels and weigh them against the benefits, although the metrics for doing so are still fuzzy and ill-defined.
	- Define best practices, training, and regulation that encourage responsible uses and discourage or ban concealed or harmful ones; the current largely laissez-faire approach is unlikely to produce this.
- **Adoption pressures**: competition creates a "prisoner's dilemma" pushing individuals and organisations to adopt AI hastily without deliberate evaluation of costs, benefits, or why the technology should be developed at all. Society has thereby de facto made a "Faustian bargain", granting AI access to data, cognitive workflows, and decisions in exchange for efficiency and less tedious effort. Discussion has thus shifted pragmatically to managing coexistence.[^4]
- **Costs** (cf. [[Ethical Concerns of Artificial Intelligence]]):[^5]
	- The business climate seeks a *Wunderwaffe* optimised for power and breadth in the hope of outrunning its problems, and deploys AI in every sphere regardless of benefit to end users, alienating and frustrating people.
	- Massive upfront investment drives a profit-driven model and a new technological elite capturing finite resources (land, energy, water, skilled labour), analogous to Gilded Age robber barons, producing inequities in economic stability, access, and quality of life.
	- Significant environmental costs; no large model has yet offset its own resource consumption and emissions.
	- Exacerbation of existing inequalities, or creation of new ones.
	- Privatised models competing for investment and users are incentivised to develop "spiky" capabilities for competitive advantage rather than consistent performance across domains.
	- Dependence on human-generated data risks [[Model Collapse|AI collapse]].
- **Equitable access**: many research benefits do not need the most advanced models; smaller models can be distilled from larger ones with small, targeted, user-defined training libraries. A publicly funded, publicly accessible AI resource or coalition could serve those left behind by corporatised models.[^6]
- **Use as seasoning**: AI should be an optional light touch in cognitive workflows, "like a small splash of vanilla", enriching the work without overwhelming it; AI content used as the core component of a workflow yields poor outcomes. As capability and adoption grow, however, the ability to opt out will diminish.[^7]
- In the medium term, AI should be treated as a tool or junior partner, rather than a replacement, for human-centred work.[^8]
