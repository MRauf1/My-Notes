# Definition
> [!info] Goal-Setting Theory (Locke and Latham)
> Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy goals or vague "do your best" goals, provided there is commitment, feedback and enough ability. It is one of the best-replicated findings in organizational psychology, with effects of roughly $d \approx 0.5$–$0.8$ (Locke and Latham, 2002).

# Properties
- **Mechanisms.** Goals direct attention, mobilize effort, increase persistence and prompt the search for strategies.
- **Moderator.** On complex, novel tasks a specific *performance* goal can hurt, and a *learning* goal (master the strategies) works better.
- **Clear's critique ("goals vs. systems").** Results come from systems, not goals.[^1]
	- Winners and losers share the same goals (survivorship bias).
	- Achieving a goal is only a momentary change.
	- Goals defer happiness ("once I reach my goal, I'll be happy") and create an either-or conflict.
	- Goals leave nothing to push you forward once achieved.
	- "You do not rise to the level of your goals. You fall to the level of your systems."
- **Evidence check.** Clear concedes that goals are good for setting direction and systems for making progress. His "forget about setting goals" goes beyond the evidence.[^2] Strategies such as [[Implementation Intention|implementation intentions]] work by attaching a plan to a goal, so goals and systems complement each other. The survivorship-bias point is valid as logic.
- **Near-term subgoals.** Children given near-term subgoals in a math program progressed faster and gained more self-efficacy and interest than those given only a distant goal (Bandura and Schunk, 1981). This is Goggins' chunking: commit only to the next landmark (see [[Central Governor Model]]).
- **"Goals Gone Wild" (Ordóñez et al., 2009).** Goal setting works far less well outside the lab. Specific, challenging goals can narrow focus so that unmeasured but important aspects are neglected, encourage risk-taking and unethical behaviour, and erode intrinsic motivation; in personal life, one can attain goals at the expense of ruining one's life. Locke and Latham (2009) replied that these harms reflect misapplication, not goal setting itself.[^antidote]
- **Goals and identity.** The more people fixate on an endpoint, the more it becomes part of their identity, so that failure feels like an attack on who they are and abandoning the goal becomes harder (Kayes's analysis of the 1996 Everest disaster).[^antidote]
- **Goals as anxiety relief.** Much goal investment is driven by [[Intolerance of Uncertainty]] rather than the value of planning. Taxi drivers who quit once they hit a daily income target (Camerer et al., 1997) were read this way, though later analyses found weaker reference-dependence (Farber, 2015).[^antidote]
- **The Yale goals study is a myth.** The claim that the 3% of Yale's class of 1953 with written goals out-earned the other 97% combined is a legend; no such study took place. Its spread shows how far the fascination with goals has gone, not that goals are useless.[^antidote]
- **Alternatives.** Hold goals loosely: a broad sense of direction without a fixed vision ("meandering with purpose"), "non-maximising" customs that stop one goal from crowding out others (Bateson on Balinese villages), and [[Effectuation]].[^antidote]
- Related: [[SMART Goal]], [[Process]], [[Lead Measure]], [[Mental Contrasting]].
