# Definition
> [!info] Friction (Sliding Friction)[^1]
> The [[Force]] resisting the relative sliding of two solid surfaces in contact, empirically proportional to the normal force $N$ pressing the surfaces together:
> $$
> \begin{align}
> F = \mu N
> \end{align}
> $$
> where $\mu$ is the coefficient of friction.

# Properties
- An approximate, empirical law rather than a fundamental one: $\mu$ is not truly constant, varies with surface cleanliness and contamination, and the law breaks down entirely at high normal force or high sliding speed (excessive heat generation).
- Originates at the atomic scale from countless points of contact where atoms of the two surfaces momentarily cling together and then snap apart, generating vibration and ultimately heat — not, as once thought, from simply lifting one surface over the bumps of the other, which alone would dissipate no energy.
- Static friction (needed to start sliding) and sliding friction (needed to maintain it) are, for dry metals, very difficult to distinguish experimentally; apparent differences are usually artifacts of lubricants or flexible supports.
- Reported tables of $\mu$ for a given pair of pure metals are unreliable, since real surfaces are never the pure metal itself but a layer of oxides and other contaminants; two surfaces of a genuinely identical, uncontaminated metal will not slide at all, but stick together — see [[Cold Welding]].
- Molecular forces between atoms (see [[Van der Waals Force]]) are the ultimate origin of friction.
