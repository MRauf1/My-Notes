# Definition
> [!info] Conservation of Momentum[^1]
> The total [[Momentum]] of an isolated system of particles does not change over time:
> $$
> \begin{align}
> m_1\mathbf{v}_1 + m_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots = \text{constant}
> \end{align}
> $$
> provided no net external [[Force]] acts on the system.

# Properties
- A direct consequence of [[Newton's Third Law]] together with [[Newton's Second Law]]: for any pair of interacting particles, their mutual (internal) forces are equal and opposite at every instant, so $d\mathbf{p}_1/dt = -d\mathbf{p}_2/dt$ and hence $d(\mathbf{p}_1+\mathbf{p}_2)/dt = 0$; summed over any number of particles, all internal forces cancel in pairs, leaving only external forces able to change the system's total momentum.
- The sum of all external forces on a system equals the rate of change of the system's total momentum — a generalization of [[Newton's Second Law]] to a system of many particles.
- In special relativity, momentum is still conserved, but the momentum of each particle is given by [[Relativistic Momentum]] rather than the Newtonian $m\mathbf{v}$.
- In quantum mechanics, momentum is no longer simply $m\mathbf{v}$ — velocity is not even well-defined for a particle represented as a wave, and momentum is instead related to the wave's spatial frequency — but the total momentum of an isolated system remains conserved nonetheless.
- Momentum can be carried by an [[Electromagnetic Field|electromagnetic field]] itself, not only by particles — see [[Electromagnetic Field Momentum]] — which is required for momentum to remain exactly conserved once interactions are not transmitted instantaneously.

[^1]: [The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Vol. I, Ch. 10: Conservation of Momentum](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_10.html)
