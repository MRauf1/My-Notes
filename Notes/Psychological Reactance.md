# Definition
> [!info] Psychological Reactance[^1]
> An aversive motivational state that arises when people perceive a threat to their behavioral freedom. It drives them to restore that freedom, often by doing the opposite of what they were pressured to do (Brehm, 1966). It explains why Carnegie warns never to say "You're wrong" and never to announce "I am going to prove so-and-so to you": both "arouse opposition and make the listener want to battle with you before you even start."

# Properties
- **Evidence.** Reactance is anger plus counter-arguing (Dillard and Shen, 2005). Health messages with controlling language ("you must", "you need to") are less persuasive than autonomy-supportive ones (Miller et al., 2007).
- **Orders versus questions.** Asking questions, giving suggestions and offering choices preserves autonomy and reduces reactance.[^2] Carnegie's examples are Owen D. Young, who "gave suggestions, not orders", and the park ranger whose commands were obeyed "sullenly and with resentment."[^3]
- **Signaling openness.** "I may be wrong. Let's examine the facts."
	- Franklin dropped "certainly" for "it so appears to me at present" and found his opinions "a readier reception."[^4]
	- Expressing *receptiveness to opposing views* makes others see you as more persuasive and trustworthy and reduces conflict (Minson, Chen and Tinsley, 2020; Yeomans et al., 2020).
