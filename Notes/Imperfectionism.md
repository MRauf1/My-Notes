# Definition
> [!info] Imperfectionism (Burkeman)[^1]
> The outlook that your limitations are not obstacles to a meaningful life, to be overcome on the way to some imagined point of fulfillment. Accepting them and stepping more fully into them is how you build a saner, freer, more accomplished, more connected life. A central tenet: the day when everything else is "out of the way" never comes, so for finite humans the time for a meaningful life has to be now.

# Properties
- **Facts of finitude.** You will never get on top of everything, feel fully confident about the future, or fully understand other people. The list of worthwhile things you could do will always be far longer than the time you have. You will always be vulnerable to disasters and distressing emotions, and have only partial influence over how your time unfolds.[^2]
- These truths are what free you to act. Give up trying to do everything and you can put your finite time into a few things that count. Stop demanding perfection and you can plunge into your work and relationships. Stop making sanity depend on control and you can start enjoying life now.[^3]
- **Impossible vs. difficult.** Realizing that a struggle you treated as very difficult is actually impossible (e.g., getting through an effectively infinite to-do list) brings relief. It is like giving up trying to stay dry in a rainstorm. Anxiety subsides, and you act because things are worth doing, not to reach a utopia of perfect productivity.[^4]
- No real work will ever meet the perfect standards in your head. Even experts feel they are winging it, so you will probably make your contribution while feeling unprepared.[^5]
- **Make the burden heavier.** Zen teacher Jiyu-Kennett's approach was to make the student's burden so heavy that they put it down, i.e., to show that the situation is irredeemable and so give permission to stop struggling. "Our suffering," says Mel Weitsman, "is believing there's a way out." The real problem was never failing to find the way to control or safety, but imagining it was possible at all. Burkeman calls this a recurring Zen motif.[^6]
- "What is true is already so. Owning up to it doesn't make it worse." (Eugene Gendlin)[^7]
- It is not settling for less or passive resignation. It means bold action and creation without the background agenda of full control. Facing finitude makes it easier to spend time on worthwhile things.[^8]
- **Reflection (Bruce Tift).** Imagine that your most bothersome trait will stay with you for life. After disappointment comes relief: you needn't wait to win that struggle before diving into reality.[^9]
- Nothing anyone has ever done needed superhuman capacities. Acknowledging limits frees time, energy, and psychological freedom to accomplish a great deal.[^10]
