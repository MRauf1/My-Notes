# Definition
> [!info] Cognitive Reappraisal[^1]
> An emotion-regulation strategy that changes how a situation is *interpreted* in order to change its emotional impact (Gross's process model). Clear's version: "You don't 'have' to. You 'get' to." Both readings are true, and we can find evidence for whichever mindset we choose.

# Properties
- **Evidence.** A meta-analysis of 190 experiments found that reappraisal moderately improves emotional outcomes ($d \approx 0.36$) and works better than suppression (Webb, Miles and Sheeran, 2012). Reappraising smoking cues reduces cue-induced craving (Kober et al., 2010).
- **Arousal reappraisal.** Reframing pre-performance anxiety as excitement improved performance (Brooks, 2014). The evidence comes mainly from the original studies.[^2]
- **For habits.** Clear reframes hard habits around their benefits rather than their drawbacks, and bad habits around the benefits of avoiding them. Carr's [[Easyway Method]] systematically reframes every cue associated with smoking.[^3]
- Related: [[Emotion]], [[Nicotine Withdrawal]], [[Law of Craving]].

- **Stoic roots.** "A man is not hurt so much by what happens, as by his opinion of what happens"; "It is not the situations you have met that have thrown you; it is what you think of these situations." Carnegie's strong claim that "our life is what our thoughts make it" overstates the case: circumstances such as poverty or illness have real effects on well-being. Appraisal changes their emotional impact.[^4] See [[Cognitive Therapy]].
