# Definition
> [!info] Rumination[^1]
> Repetitive, passive focus on one's distress and on its causes, meanings and consequences, without moving toward a solution (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1991). Worry is the future-directed counterpart. Carnegie's image for rumination about the past: "you can't saw sawdust."

# Properties
- **Response styles theory.**
	- People who respond to low mood by ruminating have longer and more severe depressive episodes than those who distract themselves or problem-solve.
	- In a prospective study, rumination predicted the onset of major depression (Nolen-Hoeksema, 2000; Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco and Lyubomirsky, 2008).
- **Abstract versus concrete processing.** Abstract "why did this happen to me?" thinking is harmful. Concrete "how did this happen, what can I do?" processing is adaptive (Watkins, 2008). This matches Carnegie's one constructive use of the past: "calmly analysing our past mistakes and profiting by them, and forgetting them."[^2]
- **Thought suppression backfires.** Carnegie notes that Dempsey did not repeat "I won't worry about the past", since that would only call it to mind. Suppressing a thought makes it rebound (Wegner et al., 1987; see [[Ironic Process Theory]]). The better route is acceptance plus engagement in something else.[^3] See [[Behavioral Activation]].
- **Treatment.** Rumination-focused CBT (Watkins et al., 2011) and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy reduce rumination and relapse in depression.
- **Sunk costs.** Holding on to grievances and losses is the emotional counterpart of the [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]].
- Related: [[Worry]], [[Perseverative Cognition Hypothesis]], [[Major Depressive Disorder]], [[Problem-Solving Therapy]].

- **Complaining.** "If you took one-tenth the energy you put into complaining and applied it to solving the problem, you'd be surprised."[^4] Repeatedly discussing problems with others (*co-rumination*) strengthens friendships but increases anxiety and depression (Rose, 2002). Venting anger also tends to increase it (Bushman, 2002). Treat the cause, not only the symptom: Pausch's example is that stress is the symptom and debt is the disease ([[Problem-Solving Therapy]]).
