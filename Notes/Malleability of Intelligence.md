# Definition
> [!info] Malleability of Intelligence[^1]
> The view that [[Intelligence|intelligence]] and other basic abilities, while starting from individual differences in genetic endowment, temperament, and aptitude, can be substantially changed through education, practice, training, and method. It is the empirical basis of the [[Growth Mindset|growth mindset]].

# Properties
- Historical opponents located fixed differences in physical traits: bumps on the skull (phrenology), the size and shape of the skull (craniology), and today genes; others pointed to differences in background, experience, training, and ways of learning.[^2]
- **Alfred Binet**, inventor of the IQ test, championed malleability: the test was designed to identify Paris schoolchildren not profiting from school so that new programs could get them back on track. In *Modern Ideas About Children*: "with practice, training, and above all, method, we manage to increase our attention, our memory, our judgment and literally to become more intelligent than we were before." It is not always those who start out smartest who end up smartest.[^1]
- **Nature and nurture** are not either–or: from conception there is constant give-and-take, and (Gilbert Gottlieb) genes require input from the environment to work properly; people have more capacity for lifelong learning and brain development than once thought.[^3]
- Robert Sternberg: the major factor in achieving expertise "is not some fixed prior ability, but purposeful engagement." Cf. [[Deliberate Practice]].[^3]
- **Potential is unknowable**: potential is the capacity to develop skills with effort and coaching over time, so one cannot foresee what years of passion, toil, and training will produce; many of the most accomplished people were considered ordinary as children or were judged by experts to have no future.[^4]
- An assessment at one point in time shows no trends, improvement, or lack of effort, and has little value for understanding ability, let alone future potential.[^5]
- Studies of world-class performers (Benjamin Bloom) found most were unremarkable as children and unpredictable even by early adolescence; continued motivation, commitment, and a support network took them to the top. Bloom: "What any person in the world can learn, almost all persons can learn, if provided with the appropriate prior and current conditions of learning."[^6]
- That some can do something with little or no training does not mean others cannot do it, sometimes better, with training; much intelligence is wasted by underestimating students' potential to develop (e.g. dumbed-down material for students repeating a grade, who then learn nothing new).[^7]
