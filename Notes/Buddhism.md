# Definition
> [!info] Buddhism[^1]
> A tradition of practice and thought founded by Gotama the Buddha that seeks direct entrance to the spiritual realm without addressing deities or other "agents". It is an ongoing, clinical investigation of reality — a microscopic examination of the very process of [[Perception|perception]] — whose aim is to pick apart the screen of delusions through which we normally view the world and so reach liberation ([[Nibbana]]).

# Types
- [[Theravada Buddhism]] — South and Southeast Asia (Sri Lanka, Thailand, Burma, Laos, Cambodia).[^2]
- [[Mahayana Buddhism]] — East Asia (China, Korea, Japan, Nepal, Tibet, Vietnam); includes Zen and Tantra.[^2]

# Properties
- Its flavor is much closer to what we would call [[Psychology|psychology]] than to theological religion.[^1]
- Its fundamental attitude is intensely empirical and anti-authoritarian ([[Empiricism]]): the Buddha offered his teaching not as dogma but as propositions for each individual to investigate for themselves.[^3]
- "Faith" means confidence — knowing something is true because you have observed it working within yourself — not belief on the authority of a book, prophet, or teacher. Likewise, morality is not ritualistic obedience to an externally imposed code (see [[Levels of Morality (Buddhism)]]).[^4]
- Liberation ([[Nibbana]]) is the goal of all Buddhist systems of practice, but the routes to it are diverse.[^5]
- All Buddhist [[Meditation|meditation]] aims at the development of awareness ([[Mindfulness]]), using concentration ([[Samadhi]]) as a tool.[^6]
- Core teachings: the [[Three Marks of Existence]] ([[Anicca]], [[Dukkha]], [[Anatta]]), the [[Three Unwholesome Roots]], the [[Threefold Training]], the [[Five Aggregates]].
- Humans have a "backward" view of life: we view impermanent things as permanent and see the cause of suffering (desire) as the source of happiness.[^7]
- Buddhists do not regard themselves as pessimists: since some pain is unavoidable, learning to deal with it is a pragmatic form of optimism.[^8]
- Those willing to describe the ultimate nature of humans call it the *Buddha nature*: pure and inherently good, but blocked "like water behind a dam" by the [[Five Hindrances|hindrances]].[^9]
- Rather than classifying thoughts as good or bad, Buddhism classifies them as [[Skillful and Unskillful Thoughts|skillful or unskillful]].[^10]
- The original teachings are preserved in the [[Tipitaka]].[^11]
- One of the major schools of [[Ancient Indian Philosophy]].
