# Definition
> [!info] Passion (Stoicism)[^1]
> (*Pathos*) An excessive, irrational emotion that is in fact a false value judgment, assigning importance (goodness or badness) to something that is [[Indifferent (Stoicism)|indifferent]]. Its root cause, for [[Epictetus]], is frustrated desire or ineffective aversion: wanting something and not getting it.

# Types
The four generic passions, by apparent value and time:[^2]

| | apparent good | apparent bad |
|---|---|---|
| future | longing (appetite) | fear |
| present | pleasure | distress |

All other passions (anger, envy, jealousy, pity, grief) are subspecies of these.

# Properties
- Since inclination always leads to action, correcting passions leads one to act more rationally and in accord with nature.[^3]
- Fear of X is the judgment that X will be bad, whereas X, being neither virtue nor vice, is neither good nor bad; "it's not death or pain that's frightening, but the fear of pain or death".[^4]
- Anger arises because we value what others take from us; place no value on it and no one can make you angry.[^5]
- Passions are habits, strengthened by each occurrence "as though you'd added fresh fuel to a fire"; see [[Stoic Exercises]].[^6]
- A soul in good condition can endure passions as a fit body endures heat and cold.[^7]
- The ideal is [[Apatheia]]; the rational alternatives are [[Eupatheia|good feelings]]; involuntary reactions are [[Propatheia|first movements]].
- The first of the [[Three Disciplines (Epictetus)]], that of desire, targets the passions.
