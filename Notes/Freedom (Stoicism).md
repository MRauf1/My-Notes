# Definition
> [!info] Freedom (Stoicism)[^1]
> ([[Epictetus]]) A person is free if everything that happens to him is in accord with his will and no one can impede him: if he lives as he wants, unconstrained, with inclinations unobstructed, desires never disappointed, and aversions always effective. This is achieved not by satisfying desires but by wanting nothing that isn't one's own.

# Properties
- "Freedom isn't a product of the satisfaction of craving, but of its suppression."[^2]
- Anyone who can be impeded, constrained, or flatters others is a slave, whether for food ("minor slaves") or for a consulship ("major slaves").[^3]
- We fear not tyrants but death, exile, and loss; whoever controls our access to what we value becomes our master; "anyone who's going to be subjugated by another person must first be subjugated for a long time by things".[^4]
- What makes one unimpeded in life is knowing how to live, as knowing how to write makes one unimpeded in writing.[^5]
- Assent, inclination, and desire are unimpeded if directed only at what is ours; the body is "subject to anything that's stronger than it".[^6]
- Freedom of will is hard-won, not a birthright; only the [[Stoic Sage|sage]] is fully free.[^7]
- The road to freedom is to submit one's inclinations to God: "He wants me to have a fever; that's what I want too."[^8]
- Diogenes was free because everything he had was "just an appendage" he would let go of, even his body.[^9]
- Grounded in the [[Dichotomy of Control]]; compare [[Existentialism]] and Frankl's inner freedom.
