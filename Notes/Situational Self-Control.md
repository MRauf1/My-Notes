# Definition
> [!info] Situational Self-Control[^1]
> Self-control achieved by choosing or changing situations so that temptations are avoided rather than resisted. "Disciplined" people are better at structuring their lives in a way that does not require heroic willpower; they spend less time in tempting situations. "It's easier to avoid temptation than resist it."

# Properties
- **Evidence.**
	- People high in trait self-control report *fewer* temptations (Hofmann et al., 2012) and are more likely to avoid tempting situations (Ent, Baumeister and Tice, 2015).
	- In Gross's process model, situation selection and modification act before an impulse arises. They were more effective than resisting the impulse in the moment (Duckworth, Gendler and Gross, 2016).
- "Self-control is a short-term strategy, not a long-term one." Energy is better spent optimizing your environment than summoning willpower each time.[^2]
- **Habits are suppressed, not erased.** Once encoded, the urge returns whenever the cues reappear, so the most practical way to break a bad habit is to reduce exposure to its cue.[^3] This matches extinction research: extinction suppresses a learned response but doesn't erase it, and the response returns when the context changes or time passes (renewal and spontaneous recovery; Bouton, 2004).
- **Cue-induced wanting.** An external trigger causes a compulsive craving to repeat a bad habit (see [[Incentive Sensitization Theory]]).[^4]
- **Autocatalysis.** Bad habits feed themselves: feeling bad leads to the habit, which leads to feeling bad.[^5]
- Clear claims that scare tactics (e.g., showing smokers blackened lungs) raise anxiety and so trigger the habit.[^6] On average the evidence says otherwise: pictorial warnings increase quit attempts (Brewer et al., 2016).
- Related: [[Willpower]], [[Choice Architecture]], [[Habit Discontinuity Hypothesis]], [[Law of Cue]].
