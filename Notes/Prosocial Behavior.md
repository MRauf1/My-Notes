# Definition
> [!info] Prosocial Behavior (Well-Being Effect)[^1]
> Voluntary action that benefits others, such as helping, giving or kindness. It reliably increases the giver's own well-being. Carnegie's Rule 7: "Forget yourself by becoming interested in others. Do every day a good deed that will put a smile of joy on someone's face."

# Properties
- **Evidence.**
	- Spending money on others made people happier than spending it on themselves (Dunn, Aknin and Norton, 2008). The effect recurs across cultures (Aknin et al., 2013). A large preregistered replication found it smaller than originally reported (Aknin et al., 2020).
	- A meta-analysis found that acts of kindness have a small-to-medium positive effect on the actor's well-being, $d \approx 0.28$ (Curry et al., 2018).
	- Volunteering is associated with lower mortality, though confounding is likely (Okun, Yeung and Brown, 2013).
- **Self-focus mechanism.** Carnegie says helping works because it "will cause us to stop thinking of ourselves: the very thing that produces worry and fear and melancholia". Self-focused attention is in fact associated with depression and anxiety (Mor and Winquist, 2002).[^2] Adler's "fourteen-day cure" is a clinical anecdote, not tested.[^3]
- **Autonomy matters.** Giving that is chosen and lets people see its impact boosts well-being more than obligated giving (Weinstein and Ryan, 2010; Aknin et al., 2013). "Doing good to others is not a duty. It is a joy." Expecting gratitude in return undermines the benefit.[^4]
- **Interest in others.** Showing genuine interest and listening makes others feel valued and builds relationships. Responsive listening increases liking (Huang et al., 2017, on question asking).[^5] See [[Social Skill]].
