# Definition
> [!info] Motivational Interviewing (MI)[^1]
> A collaborative counseling style for strengthening a person's *own* motivation to change (Miller and Rollnick). Its core methods:
> - Open questions, affirmations, reflective listening and summaries.
> - "Rolling with resistance" instead of arguing.
> - Eliciting the person's own reasons for change ("change talk") rather than supplying them.
>
> It formalizes several of Carnegie's principles: avoid arguments, let the other person do most of the talking, ask questions instead of giving orders, and let them feel the idea is theirs.

# Properties
- **Evidence.**
	- Meta-analyses find MI more effective than no treatment and about as effective as other active treatments across substance use, health behavior and treatment engagement. Effects are small overall, $g \approx 0.22$ (Lundahl et al., 2010). Effects are larger for alcohol and drug use (Hettema, Steele and Miller, 2005).
	- Arguing for change provokes *sustain talk* (the person defends the status quo), and clients who talk more against change change less (Miller and Rose, 2009).
- **The righting reflex.** People who want to help instinctively argue for change. This usually triggers the opposite argument, an instance of [[Psychological Reactance]]. "A man convinced against his will is of the same opinion still."[^2]
- **Self-persuasion.** Arguments people generate themselves are more persuasive than arguments they are given (Aronson, 1999). This is the mechanism behind "Let the other person feel that the idea is his or hers."[^3] See [[IKEA Effect]].
- **Ethics.** MI is defined by partnership and acceptance, not manipulation. Carnegie says the same: "each party should gain."[^4]
- Related: [[Active Listening]], [[Persuasion]], [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]].
