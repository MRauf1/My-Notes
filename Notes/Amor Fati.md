# Definition
> [!info] Amor Fati[^1]
> ("Love of one's fate"; Nietzsche, who was a professor of classical philology steeped in Graeco-Roman literature) The attitude of wanting nothing to be different from how it is or was. Its Stoic precursor is to accept whatever happens as if one had willed it, "will your fate". Epictetus: don't wish things to happen as you want, but want them to happen as they do happen (*Enchiridion* 8).

# Properties
- Nietzsche, *Ecce Homo*: "My formula for greatness in a human being is *amor fati*: that one wants nothing to be different, not forward, not backward, not in all eternity."
- Hadot on the sage: once he sees that indifferent things depend on universal Nature, not on his will, they take on infinite interest for him. He accepts them all with equal love and "says 'Yes!' to the entire universe and to each of its parts and events".[^2] See [[Living in Accord with Nature]].
- "If we can't get what we like, we must like what we have": instead of lamenting our lot, learn to love it (Baudouin).[^3]
- **Descartes' third maxim.** "Conquer myself rather than fortune… change my desires rather than the order of the world." After doing our best, regard what is lacking as impossible. Then we will no more regret it than not owning the kingdoms of China or Mexico, "making a virtue of necessity".[^4]
- **Not passivity.** Acceptance concerns what has *already* happened, which is unchangeable. Only the immediate future can be changed, so accepting the past or present doesn't preclude assertive action: "I can accept the fact that something has happened and yet continue to try to change it."[^5] Compare the [[Lazy Argument]].
- Rests on [[Stoic Determinism]] and the [[Dichotomy of Control]]. Contemplating determinism and accepting the inevitable (Dubois: "patience towards unavoidable events… is the only stand to take in face of the inevitable") is a recurring therapeutic theme from the Stoics to Spinoza and Dubois.[^6]
