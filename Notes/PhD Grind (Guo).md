# Review[^1]

He realized that one must have hunger for publication, not just in oneself, but it also concerns collaborators and advisors too.

Time and time again, the author meets failure after failure. Despite his best efforts, all of the discouraging events of his teammates quitting and dropping out of PhD, he still persists, but to no avail. Currently, he is in his third year, and he still has yet to publish a top-tier first author paper.

For tenured professors, they can take more risks, but their students are the ones taking the risk as well by implementing these ideas, and the failure will be minor for the professor, but potentially catastrophic for the student leading them to drop out altogether.

Funding is another big source of problems because if you get your funding from your professor, you may be forced to specifically work under a certain topic/problem in order to get the funding.

Connections are extremely important. Make them and use them as much as possible.

You need to have knowledge of the field itself, like the buzzwords, trends, motivations, and so on. These small details can make the difference between your papers getting accepted or rejected, and these small details take time and experience to develop.

If you cannot discipline yourself, setting strict deadlines, such as registering to publish at a conference, are good ways to keep you focused on your work and keep you from procrastinating.

It's interesting how despite his repeated failures, his advisor didn't stop working with him or critisizing him or anything like that. I wonder what portion of advisors are pretty understanding of students' struggles and can empathize.

You've got to be proactive. Even if it doesn't end in immediate results like you had hoped, your efforts may end up benefitting you down the line in a few years in very unexpected ways.

When doing research with others, it can be very helpful in a lot of ways to align with the subjective preferences of your collaborators, especially advisors/professors.

PhD can develop you into a more resilient person similar to how finishing an Ironman would too. But it can also lead to burnout and worsen you instead.

His lessons learned:
1) Results trump intentions.
2) Outputs trump inputs.
	1) Don't just spend your entire time with classes and reading papers; use sources/inputs as a way to guide your output. Reading just inputs without producing anything new is just a consumption binge.
3) Find relevant information.
4) Create lucky opportunities
	1) By being proactive, putting your work on display, connecting with others, and so on.
5) Play the game.
6) Lead from below.
7) Professors are human.
8) Be well-liked.
9) Pay some dues.
10) Reject bad defaults.
11) Know when to quit.
12) Recover from failures.
13) Ally with insiders.
14) Give many talks.
15) Sell, sell, sell.
16) Generously provide help.
17) Ask for help.
18) Express true gratitude.
19) Ideas beget ideas.
20) Grind hard and smart.

The author didn't find the journey consistently fun, but despite all of the challenges, it was one of his most fulfilling experiences in life.
