# Definition
> [!info] Steeling Effect[^1]
> Experiencing manageable stress or adversity can increase resilience to later stress (Rutter). Goggins calls this "callousing the mind": negative experiences, deliberately sought discomfort and doing something that sucks every day build tolerance for suffering.

# Properties
- **Evidence (U-shape).** People with *some* lifetime adversity reported better mental health and well-being than both those with none and those with a lot (Seery, Holman and Silver, 2010). The benefit belongs to moderate, manageable stress; severe adversity such as chronic childhood abuse is harmful on average (see [[Adverse Childhood Experiences]]).
- **Stress inoculation training** (Meichenbaum) prepares people with graded exposure to stressors and coping skills. A meta-analysis found it reduces anxiety and improves performance under stress (Saunders et al., 1996).
- **Appraisal matters.** If you keep seeing yourself as a victim of circumstance, the callous becomes resentment: too cautious, untrusting and angry, "but not hard of mind". Goggins reframed his past as "the ultimate training ground".[^2] Compare [[Cognitive Reappraisal]].
- **Sustainable increments.** "Moving the needle bit by bit and making those changes sustainable".[^3] Gradual exposure is also how fears are reduced in [[Exposure Therapy]].
- Related: [[Central Governor Model]], [[Stress]].

- **Hitting bottom (Carnegie).** "I've hit bottom and I've stood it. There's no place to go now but up." Surviving an agonising experience makes everyday problems seem small by comparison.[^4] It fits the U-shaped evidence above, which applies to manageable adversity only.
- **Post-traumatic growth.** Dąbrowski noted that many WWII survivors felt their suffering had made them more grateful, confident and unfazed by trivia, and argued that anxiety and sadness can be the necessary pain of growth.[^manson] Self-reported growth after trauma is common (Tedeschi and Calhoun, 1996), but it correlates poorly with measured pre-to-post change in well-being (Frazier et al., 2009; Jayawickreme et al., 2021). So reported growth is not good evidence of actual growth.
