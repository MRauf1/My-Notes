# Definition
> [!info] Hellenistic Philosophy[^1]
> The philosophical schools of the Greek and Roman world after Plato and Aristotle, usually identified as:
> 1. The Academy of Plato;
> 2. The Lyceum or Peripatetic school of Aristotle;
> 3. The Stoa, the Stoic school of Zeno ([[Stoicism]]);
> 4. The Garden of Epicurus ([[Epicureanism]]);
> 5. Scepticism, mainly associated with the Academy;
> 6. [[Cynicism]], loosely associated with the Stoics.
>
> Traces of the older Pythagorean sect also survived ([[Pythagoras]]).

# Properties
- **Shared therapeutic aim (Hadot).** All schools defined wisdom as perfect peace of mind and philosophy as a remedy for human misery. They differed on its cause: social convention (Cynics), false pleasures (Epicureans), the pursuit of pleasure and egoistic self-interest (Stoics), false opinions (Sceptics). All agreed with [[Socrates]] that misery comes from ignorance, and that evil lies in value judgements rather than in things ([[Cognitive Mediation]]).[^2]
- **Practices relevant to psychotherapy:** memorised philosophical maxims and attitudes; lifestyle changes (moderate diet, sleep, exercise, simple clothing, friendship); contemplative imagery (the [[View from Above]], rehearsing anticipated events); rhetorical exercises (reading key texts, discussion, keeping a journal: [[Hypomnemata]]).[^3]
- Most schools assumed that, with patient training, one could rise above circumstances and master desires and emotions ([[Philosophy as a Way of Life]]).[^4]
- **The Stoics as Socratics.** Zeno of Citium came to Athens after a shipwreck, a few decades after Plato's death, and read Xenophon's *Memorabilia* of Socrates. The Stoics saw themselves as more faithful to Socrates' life and ethics than the Platonists, and more moderate and socially engaged than the Cynics (the Cynics being their "more outlandish precursors"). Stoicism was thus the main sect of Socratic therapeutics, a lineage CBT's "Socratic method" rarely acknowledges.[^5]
- **Spread to Rome.** Panaetius of Rhodes, head of the Athenian Stoa, was introduced to Roman society around 140 BC by Scipio Africanus the Younger, after which many educated Romans embraced Stoicism.[^6]
- **Influence.** Epictetus, an ex-slave, shaped the thought of the emperor Marcus Aurelius. Stoic influence is traceable in Shakespeare, Montaigne, Descartes, Pascal, Spinoza, Shaftesbury and Joseph Butler.[^7]
