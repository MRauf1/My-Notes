# Definition
> [!info] Divisions of Philosophy (Stoicism)[^1]
> The Stoic division of philosophy into three fields:
> - **Logic** - rules of correct argument and thinking, plus grammar, linguistics, rhetoric, [[Epistemology|epistemology]], and all tools for discovering truth.
> - **Physics** - the nature of the world and its laws, including ontology and theology as well as physics, astronomy, and cosmology.
> - **Ethics** - how to achieve happiness, the conditions for a fulfilled, flourishing human life.

# Properties
- Some Stoics held the three equally important; others, including [[Epictetus]] of the *Discourses*, subordinated logic and physics to ethics.[^2]
- Physics is worthwhile, "but don't get lost in the details"; knowing whether things are made of atoms or fire matters less than knowing good and bad and the limits of desire. "Know yourself" means understanding one's place in the whole, as a chorus member attends to the chorus.[^3]
- Logic is not useless but not an end in itself: a tool, a measuring instrument for judging correctly; theoretical study is useless unless put into practice. Failing to follow an argument is itself a fault.[^4]
- Three aspects of philosophy (Handbook 52): (1) applying principles ("Don't lie"); (2) proofs (why one shouldn't lie); (3) analysis of proofs (what a proof, entailment, truth are). The third exists for the second and the second for the first, yet "we tell lies, while being thoroughly conversant with how to prove that we shouldn't lie".[^5]
- Chief teaching method: study of classic texts, especially Chrysippus.[^6]
- A philosopher's material is reason, his objective to reason correctly, and his principles (Zeno) the elements of reason; one is a philosopher by the art, not by the cloak and beard.[^7]
- See [[Philosophy as a Way of Life]], [[Three Disciplines (Epictetus)]], [[Propositional Logic]].
