# Definition
> [!info] Strong Artificial Intelligence[^1]
> The view that an appropriately programmed computer is not merely a tool in the study of the mind but really *is* a mind, in the sense that computers given the right programs can literally be said to understand and have other cognitive states. Consequently, the programs are not mere tools for testing psychological explanations; they are themselves the explanations.

# Properties
- Contrasted with [[Weak Artificial Intelligence]].
- Applied to story-understanding programs (e.g. Schank's), strong AI claims that (1) the machine literally understands the story, and (2) what the machine and program do explains the human ability to understand stories.[^2]
- Holds that instantiating a formal program with the right inputs and outputs is sufficient for, indeed constitutive of, [[Intentionality|intentionality]]; as Newell put it, the essence of the mental is the operation of a physical symbol system.[^3]
- Challenged by Searle's [[Chinese Room Argument]], which concludes that no program by itself is sufficient for thinking; since strong AI is about programs rather than machines, it has little to tell us about thinking ([[Biological Naturalism]]).[^4]
- Searle's diagnosis of why strong AI seemed plausible:[^5]
	- *Ambiguity of "information processing"*: people process information in the sense of meaningful content, whereas the computer merely manipulates formal symbols; it has [[Syntax|syntax]] but no [[Semantics|semantics]].
	- *Residual behaviourism or operationalism*, as embodied in the [[Turing Test]].
	- *Residual [[Dualism|dualism]]*: in strong AI (and functionalism), what matters is the program, which is independent of its realisation, so the project only makes sense if the mind is conceptually *and* empirically separable from the brain. Many AI researchers were shocked by the idea that mental phenomena might depend on the actual physical/chemical properties of brains.
- Computer simulations are not the real thing: the computer can simulate anything (storms, fires), and a simulation of a mental process is no more a mind than a simulation of a fire burns.[^6]
