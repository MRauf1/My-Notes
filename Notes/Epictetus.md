# Definition
> [!info] Epictetus[^1]
> Greek [[Stoicism|Stoic]] philosopher (c. 50–135 CE), born a slave in Hierapolis (southwestern Turkey), one of the four great Roman expositors of Stoicism alongside [[Seneca]] (an imperial courtier), [[Musonius Rufus]] (an equestrian), and Marcus Aurelius (an emperor). His teaching addresses one question: how must we live if our lives are to be subjectively fulfilling and objectively worthwhile? His answer: get ever closer to your true self.

# Properties
- Worked in the household of Tiberius Claudius Epaphroditus in Rome; even while enslaved he attended the lectures of [[Musonius Rufus]], and later set up as a teacher under Rufus's patronage. In old age (by then lame) he retired, adopted a child, and took in a housekeeper.[^2]
- His experience of slavery marked him for life: more than any other ancient philosopher he insisted the goal of life must be [[Freedom (Stoicism)|freedom]]. He held that all humans are equal by nature because all possess reason, yet was no active abolitionist, since for a Stoic one's station in life is nearly irrelevant to moral progress; his attitude toward women was conventional.[^3]
- Like [[Socrates]] and Musonius he wrote nothing; the *Discourses* and the *Handbook* (*Enchiridion*) are his student Arrian's records. The *Handbook* is Arrian's distillation with an emphasis on practice over theory, not a mere excerpt of the *Discourses*.[^4]
- Orthodox in doctrine (looking back to Chrysippus and the founders), but original in organising practical training into the [[Three Disciplines (Epictetus)|three disciplines]] of desire, inclination, and assent.[^5]
- Teaching style: shock tactics of reproof and abuse (a philosopher's duty is to make a student see his "disgraceful state"), homely analogies, rapid-fire Socratic dialogue with imaginary interlocutors, and imperatives and rhetorical questions like a street preacher; yet he did not claim perfection and often included himself in "we".[^6]
- His students were young, male, and generally well-off, as only the elite could afford higher education in rhetoric and philosophy.[^7]
- His Stoicism has a strongly religious flavour; see [[Stoic Theology]].[^8]
- Core ideas: [[Dichotomy of Control]], [[Prohairesis]], [[Assent (Stoicism)|assent]] to [[Impression (Stoicism)|impressions]], [[Indifferent (Stoicism)|indifferents]], [[Stoic Exercises]], and [[Philosophy as a Way of Life]].
- Review: [[The Complete Works (Epictetus)]].
