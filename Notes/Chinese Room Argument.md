# Definition
> [!info] Chinese Room Argument[^1]
> Searle's thought experiment against [[Strong Artificial Intelligence]]. A person who understands no Chinese is locked in a room and given batches of Chinese symbols (which outsiders call a "script", a "story", and "questions") together with rules in English (the "program") for manipulating them, and returns symbols (the "answers"). With enough skill, the answers become indistinguishable from those of a native Chinese speaker, yet the person understands nothing of Chinese. Since the person is simply an instantiation of the program, performing computational operations on formally specified elements, instantiating a program is not sufficient for understanding.

# Types
Replies to the argument and Searle's responses:
- **Systems reply** - the person does not understand, but the whole system (person, rules, paper) does. Response: let the person memorise the rules and do everything in their head; they still understand nothing, and hence neither does any subsystem. The only motivation for positing an understanding subsystem is passing the [[Turing Test]], whose adequacy is precisely at issue; and the reasoning would make all sorts of non-cognitive subsystems cognitive. Taken to its extreme (McCarthy's claim that thermostats have beliefs), it makes mind everywhere, which is fatal to strong AI as a science of what distinguishes minds from thermostats and livers.[^2]
- **Robot reply** - embed the program in a robot with perception and motor capacities. Response: this tacitly concedes that cognition is not solely formal symbol manipulation, and it adds nothing to understanding, since the same thought experiment applies to the robot's inputs and outputs.[^3]
- **Brain simulator reply** - simulate the actual sequence of neuron firings at the synapses of a Chinese speaker. Response: (a person operating water pipes could implement this) it simulates the wrong things about the brain, its formal structure rather than its causal powers to produce intentional states; and if AI required knowing how the brain works, we would not bother with AI.[^4]
- **Combination reply** - a brain-shaped robot, with a brain-simulating program, behaving indistinguishably from a human. Response: we would indeed rationally attribute [[Intentionality|intentionality]] to it as long as we knew nothing more, but that attribution rests on its looking and behaving like us, not on its program; once we knew its behaviour came from a formal program, we would withdraw it. We attribute intentionality to animals because their behaviour is coherent and they are made of similar causal stuff to us.[^5]

# Properties
- Refutes both strong-AI claims: the program does not understand (its understanding is not partial, like knowledge of a foreign language, but *zero*), and it provides no sufficient condition for, nor any reason to think it is a necessary condition of or contribution to, human understanding.[^6]
- The key difference between understanding English and manipulating Chinese is that one knows what the English symbols *mean*: formal symbol manipulations by themselves are meaningless, i.e. programs have [[Syntax|syntax]] but no [[Semantics|semantics]].[^7]
- Formal properties have no causal powers except that, when instantiated, of producing the next stage of the formalism.[^8]
- Three reasons programs cannot be minds:[^9]
	- The same program can have all sorts of crazy realisations with no intentionality.
	- Programs are purely formal, whereas intentional states are defined by their content.
	- Mental states are literally products of brain operation, whereas the program is not in that way a product of the computer.
- Does not show that machines cannot think: humans are machines that think, and anything describable as instantiating some program can think if it is such a machine. It shows only that a machine cannot think *solely in virtue of* instantiating a program ([[Biological Naturalism]]).[^10]
- Directly challenges the sufficiency of behavioural tests such as the [[Turing Test]], and is related to the [[Argument from Consciousness]].
