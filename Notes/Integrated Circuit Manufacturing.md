# Definition
> [!info] Integrated Circuit Manufacturing[^1]
> An integrated circuit (chip) is a device combining dozens to millions of transistors, where a transistor is an on/off switch controlled by an electric signal. A very large-scale integrated (VLSI) circuit contains hundreds of thousands to millions of transistors. Manufacturing begins with silicon, a natural semiconductor element, which is processed into a silicon crystal ingot, sliced into wafers, patterned with transistors and conductors, diced into individual dies, and packaged.

# Properties
- Silicon is a semiconductor: a substance that does not conduct electricity well, unlike a conductor or an insulator; special chemical processes transform tiny regions of silicon into transistors and other circuit elements.
- A silicon crystal ingot (roughly 8–12 inches in diameter, 12–24 inches long) is sliced into wafers no more than 0.1 inches thick, which undergo processing steps that create the transistors and up to eight levels of metal interconnect.
- Microscopic flaws called defects make a perfect wafer virtually impossible to manufacture; placing many independent dies on one wafer lets only the flawed dies be discarded, rather than the whole wafer, after dicing.
- Yield is the percentage of good dies out of the total dies on a wafer; good dies are bonded to the input/output pins of a package.
- Underlies [[Moore's Law|Moore's Law]]'s prediction of exponential growth in transistors per chip.
