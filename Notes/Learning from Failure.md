# Definition
> [!info] Learning from Failure[^1]
> Using unsuccessful attempts as information for future decisions. "Experience is what you get when you didn't get what you wanted." Pausch's *First Penguin Award* went each semester to the team that took the biggest gamble on new ideas while failing to achieve its goals, an award for "glorious failure".

# Properties
- **People learn less from failure than from success.** In several experiments, people learned less from failure feedback than from equivalent success feedback. Failure threatens the ego, so people tune out (Eskreis-Winkler and Fishbach, 2019). Learning from failure must be made deliberate and ego-safe. Examples are blameless postmortems ([[After-Action Review]]) and Pausch's award, which recasts failure as daring.[^2]
- **Intelligent failure.** Organizations learn most from failures that are small, early, in new territory, and hypothesis-driven (Edmondson; Cannon and Edmondson, 2005). Psychological safety, where people believe they won't be punished for mistakes, predicts team learning (Edmondson, 1999).
- **Correction: failed founders.** Pausch says start-ups often prefer CEOs with failed start-ups because they know the pitfalls.[^3] The data disagree. Entrepreneurs whose previous venture failed succeeded at about the same rate as first-timers (about 20% versus 18%). Previously *successful* founders did better, about 30% (Gompers et al., 2010). Failure alone isn't experience. What you take from it is.
- **Brick walls.** "The brick walls are there to give us a chance to show how badly we want something."[^4] See [[Grit]].
