# Definition
> [!info] Feedback Intervention Theory[^1]
> Kluger and DeNisi (1996) proposed that feedback works or fails depending on where it directs attention. Feedback that focuses on the **task** (what to do differently) tends to improve performance. Feedback that draws attention to the **self** (one's worth, ability or standing) tends to reduce it. This is the evidence-based version of Carnegie's Principle 1, "Don't criticize, condemn or complain": criticism "puts a person on the defensive" and "wounds a person's precious pride."

# Properties
- **Evidence.** In a meta-analysis of 607 effects, feedback improved performance on average ($d \approx 0.41$), but over one-third of feedback interventions *decreased* performance (Kluger and DeNisi, 1996). Praise and criticism that target the person rather than the task were among the least effective.
- **Criticism of the act versus the person.** The answer to "then how do you stop someone doing something bad?" is not silence. It is to criticize *specific behavior*, without judging character. Relationship research makes the same distinction between a complaint about behavior and criticism of the person (see [[Four Horsemen (Gottman)]]).[^2]
- **How to deliver it.** Carnegie's Part 4 has these rules:
	- Begin with honest appreciation.
	- Call attention to mistakes indirectly. For example, use "and" rather than "but" after praise, so the praise isn't discounted.
	- Talk about your own mistakes first.
	- Ask questions instead of giving orders.
	- Let the person save face.
	- Make the fault seem easy to correct.
	- These rules keep feedback away from the self.[^3]
	- Most of them have not been tested individually. Their common principle, reducing threat to self-image, is well supported (self-affirmation reduces defensiveness; Cohen and Sherman, 2014).
- **The "feedback sandwich".** Wrapping criticism in praise is widely taught. Evidence that it improves performance is weak. It can make the praise seem insincere, which is Carnegie's own warning about "but".[^3]
- **Self-justification.** Wrongdoers rarely blame themselves. Carnegie's examples are Capone, Crowley and Schultz.[^4] This fits the *self-serving bias*, which attributes failures to circumstances and successes to oneself (Mezulis et al., 2004). See [[Motivated Reasoning]].
- **Unsent letters.** Lincoln wrote but never mailed a harsh letter to Meade, which relieved his feelings without provoking self-justification.[^5] Compare [[Expressive Writing]].
- Related: [[Process Praise]], [[After-Action Review]], [[Operant Conditioning]].

- **Critics who care (Pausch, 2008).** "When you're screwing up and nobody says anything to you anymore, that means they've given up on you." A mentor told Pausch: "it's such a shame that people *perceive* you as being so arrogant, because it's going to limit what you're going to be able to accomplish." That framing made Pausch receptive. It describes how others see a behavior and its cost to his own goals, rather than condemning his character.[^6] See [[Peer Assessment]].
