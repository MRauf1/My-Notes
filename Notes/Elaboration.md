# Definition
> [!info] Elaboration[^1]
> A learning method in which you actively think about the meaning of what you read: how it could inform different questions and topics and how it connects with other knowledge. Ahrens calls it the best-researched and most successful learning method, the opposite of mere reviewing (Stein et al., 1984), and close to what taking smart notes and connecting them involves.

# Properties
- Learning requires effort: you must think to understand and actively retrieve old knowledge to connect it with new ideas. To a degree, learning *is* understanding, and improves only if you test yourself.[^2]
- Things you understand are easier to remember because they are connected through rules, theories, narratives, logic, mental models, or explanations. Building such connections is the point of a [[Zettelkasten]].[^3]
- **Thinking beyond the frame** (Lonka). Experienced readers read with questions in mind and relate a text to other approaches, spotting its limits and what is left unsaid. Inexperienced readers adopt the text's question and frame. Worse still is failing to place particular information within the text's larger argument.[^4]
- **Contrast over sorting.** Too much order can impede learning; deliberate variation and contrast help. Elaborating on similarities and differences between notes, instead of sorting them by topic, also improves the ability to classify (cf. [[Interleaving]]).[^5]
- Related: [[Literature Note]], [[Permanent Note]], [[Deliberate Practice]], [[Generation Effect]].
