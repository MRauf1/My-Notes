# Definition
> [!info] Socratic Irony[^1]
> [[Socrates]]' professed ignorance: his ironic refusal to consider himself wise or a sage. Psychologically it was empowering rather than self-demeaning. It kept him from taking himself too seriously or getting stuck in one viewpoint, and gave him great freedom and flexibility of thought.

# Properties
- **Prepared for refutation.** By accepting himself as flawed and unwise from the outset, he was unafraid of being proved wrong on the most important matters ([[Intellectual Humility]]).[^2]
- **Parallel to Ellis.** REBT's unconditional self-acceptance: accept yourself as a fallible human being and drop unreasonable self-demands, including the demand always to be right. This frees one from a rigid attitude that inevitably produces emotional tension ([[Self-Acceptance]], [[Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy]]).[^1]
- **Against perfectionism.** Perfectionism is self-defeating and leads to performance anxiety and paralysis. Philosophers, who deal in absolutes, easily become intellectually rigid. Socrates was comfortable with incomplete arguments and valued the process of philosophising over producing theories, schools or books.[^3]
- **Resolving the sage paradox.** The ideal of perfect wisdom places huge demands on its follower. By accepting that no mortal can attain absolute wisdom, Socrates "lifted a burden from his mind that allowed him to function more freely" ([[Stoic Sage]], [[Contemplation of the Sage]]).[^4]
- Related: [[Socratic Method]].
