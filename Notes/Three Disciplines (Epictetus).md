# Definition
> [!info] Three Disciplines (Epictetus)[^1]
> [[Epictetus]]'s original division of practical Stoic training into three progressive domains (*topoi*) in which a person must be trained to be free and truly good:
> 1. **Desire and aversion** - so that one never fails to get what one desires nor meets what one wants to avoid.
> 2. **Inclination and disinclination** (appropriate action) - so that one acts in an orderly, well-reasoned way in all one's roles.
> 3. **Assent** (judgment) - so that one is never deceived or carried away by impressions.

# Properties
- A progressive course: "first", "second", "third" (or "last"), moving from one to the next.[^1]
- **First domain** - the most urgent, because frustrated desire is the source of all [[Passion (Stoicism)|passions]] (turmoil, grief, fear, envy); its mastery makes one almost a complete philosopher and brings inner tranquillity. Beginners should abolish desire for now (since the proper objects of desire, the virtues, are not yet in reach) and redirect aversion to what is contrary to nature *among the things up to us*.[^2]
- **Second domain** - since inclination leads to action, it supplies the tools for [[Appropriate Action (Stoicism)|appropriate action]] toward gods, parents, siblings, children, and fellow citizens, not being "unfeeling as a statue". Inclinations are to be used with a light touch, with reservation ([[Reserve Clause]]), and so that each is conditional, serves the common good, and is proportionate to its object's value.[^3]
- The first two constitute ethics, the training of character (inner tranquillity plus appropriate outward behaviour).[^4]
- **Third domain** - for those already progressing; rigorous training in logic turns the true beliefs of the first two into secure knowledge, by observing how one's mind works so as not to assent too readily. Its aim is unassailability: that no untested impression slips past one's guard "even when asleep, drunk, or in a black mood", making all value judgments coherent and consistent.[^5]
- Epictetus criticised philosophers of his day for studying only the third domain (changing arguments, hypotheticals, the Liar paradox) while ignoring the first two, "hoisting your topsails" while the ship sinks.[^6]
- The "musculature of a philosopher": desire never disappointed, aversion always effective, appropriate inclination, scrupulous aspiration, circumspect assent.[^7]
- They correspond to [[Divisions of Philosophy (Stoicism)|physics, ethics, and logic]] and are cultivated by [[Stoic Exercises]].
