# Definition
> [!info] Facial Feedback Hypothesis[^1]
> Bodily expressions, especially facial ones, feed back to influence the emotions we feel. It descends from William James's view that "action seems to follow feeling, but really action and feeling go together". On that view we can regulate feeling indirectly by regulating action: "the sovereign voluntary path to cheerfulness… is to sit up cheerfully and to act and speak as if cheerfulness were already there."

# Properties
- **Replication history.**
	- The classic pen-in-mouth study (Strack, Martin and Stepper, 1988) failed a 17-lab registered replication (Wagenmakers et al., 2016).
	- A meta-analysis of 286 effects found a small but real effect, $d \approx 0.2$ (Coles, Larsen and Lench, 2019).
	- The 19-country Many Smiles collaboration found that posed smiles slightly increased happiness, but the pen-in-mouth method did not (Coles et al., 2022).
- **Practical size.** Acting "as if" happy produces a small mood shift, not a transformation. Related claims about expansive "power poses" changing hormones failed to replicate (Ranehill et al., 2015). Only small effects on *feelings* of power remain.
- **Stronger behavioral routes.** The broader idea that changing action changes feeling has better support through other mechanisms:
	- [[Behavioral Activation]] for depression.
	- [[Exposure Therapy]] for fear.
	- Self-perception, where we infer our attitudes from our behavior (Bem, 1972).
	- Carnegie's "act as if you were interested in your job" fits these better than facial feedback.[^2]
- Related: [[Emotion]], [[Cognitive Reappraisal]], [[Happiness]], [[William James]].

- **Smiling at others (Carnegie, Principle 2).** Smiles increase perceived warmth and trustworthiness. Listeners can hear a smile in the voice (Tartter, 1980), which supports Carnegie's "smile when talking on the phone."[^3] A forced smile ("force yourself to smile") draws on the same small facial-feedback effect.
