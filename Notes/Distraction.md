Constant switching from low-stimuli activities to high-stimuli activities at the slightest hint of boredom/cognitive challenge teaches your mind to never tolerate the absence of novelty.[^1]

Simple strategy of "detoxing" for a short period of time is not enough to combat the negative effects of distraction. Instead, one must learn to embrace boredom.

# Distraction as Avoidance (Burkeman)
Usually we aren't dragged away from rapt concentration. We are eager for any excuse to escape how uncomfortable it feels to face our limited control over a meaningful but demanding task. We are collaborators with the "war for our attention", and Mary Oliver calls the inner urge "the intimate interrupter".[^2] Digital detoxes limit access to distractions but don't address the urge itself. The most effective approach is to stop expecting focus to feel otherwise and to turn attention to the reality of the situation rather than railing against it.[^3]

Meditation teacher Shinzen Young found that ice-cold water hurt less the more fully he attended to the sensations.[^4] Evidence supports this: in cold-pressor experiments, acceptance-based strategies increase pain tolerance compared with suppression (meta-analysis, Kohl et al., 2012).
