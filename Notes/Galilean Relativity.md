# Definition
> [!info] Galilean Relativity[^1]
> The laws of physics take the same form in every [[Frame of Reference]] moving at constant velocity relative to any other — an observer moving in a straight line at uniform speed cannot detect that motion by any mechanical experiment performed entirely within their own frame.

# Properties
- A consequence of [[Newton's Second Law]]: since force depends only on the rate of change of [[Momentum]] (i.e. on acceleration), and a uniform velocity contributes no acceleration, adding the same constant velocity to every particle in a system leaves all forces, and hence all predicted motions, unchanged.
- Distinguished from Einstein's [[Special Relativity|(special) relativity]], which extends the same basic idea — that the laws of physics are identical in every uniformly-moving frame — to electromagnetism, and requires replacing the ordinary (Galilean) transformation between frames with the [[Lorentz Transformation]], abandoning the notion of absolute, frame-independent time and space.
- Frames related by a constant relative velocity, with no rotation or acceleration, are called inertial frames; Galilean relativity states that no mechanical experiment can distinguish one inertial frame from another.

[^1]: [The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Vol. I, Ch. 10: Conservation of Momentum](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_10.html)
