# Definition

...

Emotions of all kinds, be it positive or negative, are necessary for one's well-being. Thus, they need to be expressed in a healthy manner.

Emotions are not always representative of reality.

Emotions are body's reaction to the mind.[^1]

# Tolle's Perspective
- Emotion arises where mind and body meet. If thought and emotion conflict, the emotion tells the truth about your current state of mind, so watching emotions can reveal unconscious thought patterns.[^2]
- Thought and emotion feed each other in a vicious circle. Feeling the emotion's energy without analyzing it breaks the loop ([[Watching the Thinker]]).[^3]
- All emotions are modifications of one primordial "pain" that comes from losing awareness of who you are beyond form. Emotions are dualistic, unlike the [[States of Being]].[^4]
- Unfelt emotions linger and accumulate into the [[Pain-Body]].[^5]
- *A New Earth*: an instinctive response is the body's direct response to an external situation; an emotion is the body's response to a thought, or to an event seen through the filter of thought. Unconscious assumptions create emotions, which generate mind activity and reactions, so they create your personal reality.[^6]
- Most bodies are under constant stress generated from within the mind by the ego's compulsive thinking.[^7]

# Functional View (Manson)
- Emotions are biological feedback signals nudging us toward beneficial change: negative emotions are a call to action, positive ones a reward for proper action. Just because something feels good doesn't mean it is good, so emotions should be questioned rather than repressed or blindly obeyed.[^manson] This matches functional accounts of emotion (Keltner and Gross, 1999) and the affect-as-information model (Schwarz and Clore, 1983).
- **Correction: social and physical pain.** Manson says the brain registers little difference between physical and psychological pain.[^manson] Early imaging suggested overlapping activity for social exclusion and physical pain (Eisenberger, Lieberman and Williams, 2003), but multivariate analyses show the neural patterns are distinct (Woo et al., 2014). The overlap is partial, not identity.
- See [[Experiential Avoidance]].
