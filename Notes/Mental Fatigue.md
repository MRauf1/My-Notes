# Definition
> [!info] Mental Fatigue[^1]
> A psychobiological state caused by prolonged demanding cognitive activity. People feel tired, and they become less willing to exert control and less able to sustain performance. Carnegie claimed the opposite: "mental work alone can't make you tired", because blood leaving an active brain shows no "fatigue toxins", so fatigue must come from emotions (boredom, worry, resentment) and the muscle tension they produce.

# Properties
- **What Carnegie got right.**
	- The brain's overall energy use barely changes with mental effort. It consumes about 20% of the body's energy at rest, and focused tasks raise this by only a few percent (Raichle and Gusnard, 2002). Mental fatigue is not the brain running out of fuel the way a muscle does.
	- The "glucose depletion" account of self-control has not held up. [[Willpower]] as a depletable resource (ego depletion) largely failed a large preregistered multi-lab replication (Hagger et al., 2016).
	- Motivation and emotion strongly modulate fatigue. Rewards and interest can undo fatigue effects on performance (Boksem and Tops, 2008). Boredom makes people feel tired (see [[Boredom]]).[^2]
- **What Carnegie got wrong.** Mental work alone *does* produce fatigue, with measurable behavioral and neural signatures.
	- After about 6 hours of hard cognitive work, glutamate was about 8% higher in the lateral [[Prefrontal Cortex|prefrontal cortex]] than after easy work. The fatigued group also made about 10% more impulsive economic choices (Wiehler et al., 2022). The proposed mechanism is metabolite accumulation, not fuel depletion.
	- Prolonged cognitive tasks reduce subsequent physical endurance (Marcora, Staiano and Manning, 2009) and cognitive performance, mostly by increasing perceived effort.
	- Waking activity does produce by-products that build up in the brain. Adenosine builds sleep pressure, and metabolic waste is cleared mainly during [[Sleep]] (Xie et al., 2013). So the "no fatigue toxins" claim is outdated.
- **Opportunity-cost account.** One influential theory treats the feeling of mental effort and fatigue as a signal that the current task is costing more than alternative uses of cognition (Kurzban et al., 2013). This explains why boring tasks feel tiring and interesting ones do not, and it partly vindicates Carnegie's emphasis on interest.
- **Breaks.** "Rest before you get tired" is reasonably supported: micro-breaks of 10 minutes or less increase vigor and reduce fatigue (Albulescu et al., 2022).[^3] Short naps improve alertness: a roughly 26-minute cockpit nap improved pilot performance and alertness (Rosekind et al., 1995). Carnegie's claim that a 1-hour nap plus 6 hours of night sleep beats 8 hours of unbroken sleep has no support.[^4]
- Related: [[Attention Restoration Theory]], [[Progressive Muscle Relaxation]], [[Flow State]].
