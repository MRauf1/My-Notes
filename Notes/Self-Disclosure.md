# Definition
> [!info] Self-Disclosure[^1]
> Revealing information about oneself to others. People find it intrinsically rewarding: disclosing about the self activates brain reward regions, and people will give up money for the chance to do it (Tamir and Mitchell, 2012). This is the mechanism behind Carnegie's "Talk to people about themselves and they will listen for hours" (Disraeli).

# Properties
- **Evidence.**
	- About 30–40% of everyday speech is spent telling others about one's own experiences (Tamir and Mitchell, 2012).
	- Carnegie's claim that we spend "about 95 percent of our time thinking about ourselves" is unsupported; it was his own estimate.[^2] Carnegie also cites a telephone-company study finding that "I" is the most frequent word in conversation.[^2] That study cannot be traced, though "I" is indeed among the most frequent words in spoken English.
- **Asking questions increases liking.** People who asked more questions, especially follow-up questions, were liked more by conversation partners and got more second dates (Huang et al., 2017). Carnegie: "To be interesting, be interested. Ask questions that other persons will enjoy answering."[^3]
- **Reciprocity and closeness.** Disclosers are liked more, and people like those to whom they have disclosed (Collins and Miller, 1994). Escalating mutual disclosure creates closeness quickly (Aron et al., 1997, the "36 questions").
- **Letting others excel.** Colleagues resented one woman's boasting. When she listened instead, they shared their own successes.[^4] Unsolicited self-promotion tends to reduce liking (Scopelliti, Loewenstein and Vosgerau, 2015).
- Related: [[Active Listening]], [[Status Motive]], [[Social Skill]].
