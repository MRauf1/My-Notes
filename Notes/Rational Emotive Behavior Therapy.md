# Definition
> [!info] Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (Ellis)[^1]
> (REBT) A form of cognitive therapy built on the Stoic insight that emotional disturbance comes from beliefs about events, not the events themselves. Its core target is *absolutist demands*: elevating what we would prefer ("I want their approval") into what we believe we must have ("I must have it"), so that not getting it seems catastrophic. Therapy replaces rigid demands with flexible preferences.

# Properties
- **ABC model.** An Activating event leads to emotional and behavioural Consequences only through Beliefs about it. "There is nothing either good or bad, but thinking makes it so" (Hamlet).[^2]
- **Awfulising.** Calling an undesirable event "awful" implies it is 100% bad, yet nothing can be, since it could always be worse. Distinguish *very bad* from *completely terrible*: we respond with blind terror only to the absolutely horrific, while all other fears are finite and so can be coped with.[^3] Compare [[Decatastrophizing]].
- "The worst thing about any event is usually your exaggerated belief in its horror." Accepting that the universe is uncontrollable makes one far less anxious.[^3]
- **Shame-attacking exercises.** Deliberately doing something mildly embarrassing in public. It is the Stoic premeditation of evils ([[Stoic Exercises]]) made real: one discovers that the feared consequences don't match the facts, partly because other people are too busy thinking about themselves ([[Spotlight Effect]]).[^4] This is a form of [[Exposure Therapy]].
- **Unconditional self-acceptance.** REBT rejects global self-rating, including high [[Self-Esteem]]. Giving the "self" one rating assumes a unitary self and creates the possibility of rating it poorly; it overgeneralises across strengths and weaknesses. Rate individual acts, not yourself (Hauck; Ellis).[^5]
- Parallels the Buddhist distinction between preference and [[Non-Attachment|attachment]].[^6]
- **Evidence.** A meta-analysis of 50 outcome studies found REBT moderately effective across clinical and non-clinical problems ($d \approx 0.58$) (David et al., 2018). Irrational beliefs correlate with distress (Visla et al., 2016).
- Precursor of Beck's [[Cognitive Therapy]] and part of the [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]] family.
# Stoic Parallels (Robertson)
- **ABC in detail.** People describe their emotions in stimulus–response terms ("A causes C"), so the first step of therapy is to make them aware of the role of B. Dryden and Ellis stress that cognition, emotion and behaviour are interdependent and interact with the social and material environment. ABC is a simplified working model for clients, sufficient for practice ([[Cognitive Mediation]]).[^rob1]
- **Self-talk.** "One may control one's emotions by changing the internalized sentences, or self-talk, with which one largely created these emotions" (Ellis, 1962). Hence suggestion is countered by insight and disputation, not by positive thinking ([[Autosuggestion]], [[Self-Talk]]).[^rob2]
- **Flexible preference and the reserve clause.** Clients should feel strongly about succeeding without raising desires to absolutistic demands ("I must succeed or else I am worthless!"). "I must succeed, failure would be awful!" becomes "I strongly prefer to succeed, but even if I fail I will accept myself fully". This is the Stoic [[Reserve Clause]]; see also [[Frustration Tolerance]].[^rob3]
- **Shame-attacking and Cynic shamelessness.** Ellis's exercises (e.g. repeatedly stopping a bus without getting on) recall the more extreme Cynic practice of *anaideia* ([[Cynicism]]).[^rob4]
- Stoicism and REBT both cultivate healthy emotions rather than no emotion ([[Eupatheia]]), and both treat self-blame and blame of others as errors ([[Philosophical Empathy]], [[Socratic Irony]]).[^rob5]
