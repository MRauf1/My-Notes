# Definition
> [!info] Survivorship Bias[^1]
> (Undersampling of failure) A selection bias that arises when only units that "survived" a selection process $S$ are observed and survival depends on the outcome $Y$. Estimates computed from survivors target $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid S = 1]$ rather than the population quantity:
> $$\begin{align}\mathbb{E}[Y \mid S=1] \neq \mathbb{E}[Y], \qquad P(\text{success} \mid X) = \frac{P(X \mid \text{success})\,P(\text{success})}{P(X)},\end{align}$$
> where survivors identify only $P(X \mid \text{success})$; the denominator $P(X)$ requires sampling the failures too.

# Properties
- **Traits of the successful.** Studying only successful people (e.g. traits shared by millionaires) estimates $P(X \mid \text{success})$, but the relevant quantity for advice is $P(\text{success} \mid X)$, which needs data on those with trait $X$ who failed or never tried.[^2]
- **Variance-increasing traits.** Traits such as perseverance, risk-taking or charisma may raise the *variance* of outcomes rather than the mean, so they are common among both extreme successes and extreme failures, while people without them cluster in the middle. The very successful and very unsuccessful may have similar personalities (Denrell, 2003).[^1]
- Even the "willingness to fail" may be overvalued through survivorship bias.[^3]
- Virtually any advice on success or happiness, including the [[Backwards Law|negative path]] itself, is at risk of it, since we rarely consider those who followed the advice and failed.[^3]
- **Avoiding failure in science.** Scientists meet failure constantly but blame equipment, repeat the experiment, then shelve inexplicable results, focusing on successes (Dunbar). The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, which filters out unwanted information, seems implicated in screening out unexpected results.[^4] Compare [[Anomaly (Philosophy of Science)]] and [[Confirmation Bias]].
- Related: [[Cognitive Bias]], [[Learning from Failure]], [[Goal-Setting Theory]].
