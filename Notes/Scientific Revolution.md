# Definition
> [!info] Scientific Revolution[^1]
> A non-cumulative developmental episode in which an older [[Paradigm (Philosophy of Science)|paradigm]] is replaced, in whole or in part, by an incompatible new one: the tradition-shattering complement to the tradition-bound activity of [[Normal Science|normal science]], occurring when the profession can no longer evade [[Anomaly (Philosophy of Science)|anomalies]] that subvert existing practice.

# Properties
- A new theory is seldom just an increment to knowledge: its assimilation requires reconstructing prior theory and re-evaluating prior fact, reconstructing the field from new fundamentals, and changing the profession's view of the field, its methods, and goals. Old and new paradigms overlap largely, but never completely, in the problems they solve.[^1]
- **Why "revolution"**: like political revolutions, it begins with a sense (often in a narrow subgroup) that existing institutions have ceased to function, and paradigm choice is a choice between incompatible modes of community life. It cannot be settled by normal-scientific evaluation, since that depends on the paradigm at issue; arguments are necessarily circular, and "there is no standard higher than the assent of the relevant community", so persuasion matters alongside nature and logic.[^2]
- **Not cumulative**: out-of-date theories are not unscientific merely because discarded, so science has included beliefs incompatible with ours and development is not accretion. Modern historiography judges past science by its coherence within its own time, not by its relation to modern views.[^3]
- **Against the "special case" defence**: Newtonian mechanics seems derivable from relativity for $v \ll c$, but saving theories this way restricts them to already-observed phenomena and precision, ending research; and the derived laws are not Newton's laws, since their terms have changed meaning. Differences between successive paradigms are necessary and irreconcilable, often redefining the science.[^4]
- Paradigm debates always involve which problems are more significant to have solved, since no paradigm solves all its problems and no two leave the same ones unsolved.[^5]
- A new candidate must resolve an outstanding, recognised problem not otherwise solvable, and promise to preserve much of the concrete problem-solving ability of its predecessors; novelty for its own sake is not a desideratum in science.[^6]
- Revolutionaries are almost always very young or new to the field, less committed to the old rules and intensely focused on the crisis-provoking problems (e.g. training in another specialty).[^7]
- Resolved by conversion akin to a gestalt switch ([[Incommensurability]]), and often only generationally ([[Planck's Principle]]); rendered invisible by textbooks ([[Invisibility of Scientific Revolutions]]).
- Preceded by [[Crisis (Philosophy of Science)|crisis]]; cf. the [[Copernican Principle]] as a paradigm case.
