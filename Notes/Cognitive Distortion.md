# Definition
> [!info] Cognitive Distortion[^1]
> (Thinking error) In [[Cognitive Therapy]], a systematic, habitual error of reasoning that biases how a person interprets events and maintains emotional disturbance. Under unusual stress people readily revert to such errors.

# Types
- **Selective abstraction** ("tunnel vision", "selective thinking") - taking a detail out of context and ignoring the rest, especially discounting the positive. Beck calls it a "lie of omission" to oneself.[^2]
- **Overgeneralisation** - concluding that negative things "always" happen and positive ones "never" do.[^2]
- **Labelling** - judging things, people or events as completely bad or worthless.[^2]
- **Catastrophising / magnification** (Ellis: *awfulizing*) - exaggerating how bad something is; known in classical rhetoric as *hyperbole*. Epictetus saw it as the result of overvaluing externals.[^3]
- **Absolutist demands** (Ellis) - turning preferences into "musts" ([[Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy]]).

# Properties
- **Ancient descriptions of discounting the positive.** Plutarch: we strain the mind toward what hurts it and away from the good we have, like the Chian wine-seller who sold his good wine and drank the vinegary one, "looking for bad when surrounded by good". The remedy is to "blend" good aspects to obscure the glare of the bad. Lipsius: like flies on rough, filthy corners, "the murmuring mind… never forgets the adverse".[^4]
- The CBT remedy for selective abstraction is restoring context ("enlarging perspective"). The Stoics push this to the whole of space and time ([[View from Above]]).[^5]
- In depression, distortions cluster around the [[Cognitive Triad]].
- **Evidence.** Endorsement of cognitive distortions correlates with depression and anxiety, and decreases with successful CBT. Experimental work on *interpretation bias* shows that anxious and depressed people resolve ambiguous information more negatively. Related: [[Cognitive Bias]].
