# Definition
> [!info] Generation Effect[^1]
> Information is remembered better when you actively generate it, e.g., by producing words yourself through speaking or writing, than when you simply read the same information (Slamecka and Graf, 1978). Writing ideas down is a way of "rehearsing" them, which makes them more likely to stick.

# Properties
- **Evidence.** It is one of the most robust findings in memory research. A meta-analysis of 86 studies found an average advantage of about $d \approx 0.40$ for generated over read items (Bertsch et al., 2007).
- The benefit comes from generating, not from writing as such. Verbatim copying or highlighting is closer to rereading, which is rated a low-utility study technique (Dunlosky et al., 2013). Restating an idea in your own words, as in a [[Permanent Note]], does produce the effect.
- Closely related to the testing effect ([[Recall]]) and [[Elaboration]]. All three make learning effortful by producing the information instead of passively re-exposing yourself to it.
- Related: [[Writing]], [[Handwritten Note-Taking]], [[Long-Term Memory]].
