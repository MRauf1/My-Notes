# Definition
> [!info] AI-Assisted Mathematics[^1]
> The integration of AI tools into mathematical research, in which the ability to produce proofs increasingly becomes decoupled from the human reasoning processes traditionally needed to discover and understand them.

# Properties
- **Mathematics as sandbox**: with its old, advanced foundations and natural suitability for exploring counterfactual abstract scenarios, mathematics is a good testbed for the broader impact of AI on science and society.[^2]
- **Unlike past technologies**: earlier tools affected secondary aspects of the profession (communication, dissemination) rather than the creation of results, and did not require engaging with philosophical aspects such as knowledge, beauty, meaning, or morality. AI creates an unprecedented decoupling between the outward form of intellectual products and the values and thought processes behind them.[^3]
- **Top-down outputs**: optimising for good-looking outputs rather than underlying cognition can be surprisingly effective, the same AI making basic errors yet sometimes reaching correct answers to hard problems more accurately than experts, or giving strange but correct proofs. Reliability comes from placing AI in a rigorous environment of independent testing and verification (e.g. [[Formal Proof Assistant|formal proof assistants]]) rather than from the AI itself.[^4]
- Resolving deep conjectures remains out of reach of fully autonomous AI, but AI could plausibly soon greatly assist mathematicians, even if one hesitates to call this genuinely intelligent thought; this forces a re-evaluation of what intelligence and creativity are and how to measure them.[^4]
- The ability to explain and defend the creative process behind a result is currently a viable marker of genuine understanding distinguishing human from AI work, but if AI passes such tests too, the goalposts for intelligence and creativity may have to move again.[^5]
- **Shifting standards**: the burden of verified deductive proof may fall increasingly to computers, with tedious calculations outsourced to software. Human attention may turn to "softer" aspects such as [[Heuristic|heuristics]], motivation, strategy selection, experimental evidence, and the trial-and-error process of discovery. Standards of proof may move towards those of the natural sciences, with mathematicians adopting statistical, experimental, or even speculative-philosophical modes of argument; trusted automatic verification makes it safer to depart from the "gold standard" of rigorous deduction.[^6]
- **Division of labour**: mathematicians may specialise in stages of proposing, establishing, or interpreting results, e.g. directing AI to prove results or mining mixed human/AI literature for new directions. This continues the post-"Bourbaki era" decentralisation of mathematics.[^7]
- **Risks**:[^8]
	- The data-driven "fourth paradigm" ([[The Fourth Paradigm (Hey et al.)]]) could crowd out empirical evidence, theoretical reasoning, computational numerics, and sensory or physically grounded intuition.
	- A flood of AI-generated papers that are correct and new but contribute to no broader narrative and build no intuition.
	- Aesthetic notions like beauty and elegance may become further decoupled from formal correctness.
	- Unsettled questions of what findings to share, precedence and credit, responsibility for errors, and intellectual property of AI-generated proofs.
	- Undisclosed AI writing is widely viewed as akin to plagiarism, which ironically drives further concealment; new disclosure standards are needed.
	- Widespread circular citation loops.
- A mathematics-specific counterpart of [[AI-Assisted Scientific Research]].
