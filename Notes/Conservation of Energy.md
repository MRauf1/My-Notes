# Definition
> [!info] Conservation of Energy[^1]
> There exists a numerical quantity, energy, which does not change as an isolated system undergoes any transformation; it is an abstract mathematical invariant rather than a description of any physical mechanism or substance. To verify it, one must account for every form energy takes and for any amount entering or leaving the system.

# Properties
- Equivalent to the statement that no perpetual motion machine is possible.
- Computed by summing form-specific formulas — gravitational ([[Gravitational Potential Energy]]), [[Kinetic Energy]], heat, elastic, electrical, chemical, radiant, nuclear, and rest-mass energy ([[Mass-Energy Equivalence]]) — rather than by counting discrete "blobs" of energy, unlike the countable conserved quantities in [[Conservation of Charge]], [[Conservation of Baryon Number]], and [[Conservation of Lepton Number]].
- By [[Noether's Theorem]], follows from the time-translation invariance of the physical laws: an experiment performed today and the same experiment performed tomorrow behave identically.
- On cosmological scales this equivalence breaks down: general relativity describes an expanding universe with no global time-translation symmetry, so there is no universally agreed definition of a system's "total energy" to conserve. This — not a literal local violation — is why phenomena such as the cosmological redshift of photons or the accelerating expansion attributed to dark energy are not straightforward counterexamples; the local conservation of energy-momentum, $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$, still holds pointwise.
- Conservation of energy does not imply that all of it is available to do useful work: disordered thermal energy (e.g. in a body of water at uniform temperature) cannot be converted into ordered mechanical work without energy supplied from elsewhere. This distinction between total and *available* energy is the subject of the laws of thermodynamics and the concept of entropy for irreversible processes.
