# Review[^1]

Geniuses come up with groundbreaking results repeatedly (not all, but the best). Hence, successful research is not just pure luck. Persistance and consistency matter a great deal too.

One commonality among them is that when they were young, they had independent thoughts and the courage to pursue them. This is the necessary, but not sufficient condition.

For some people, even through failures, it can take one success to pave the path forward to more innovations. Thus, another characteristic one must have is courage and belief in yourself.

Age can be another factor. In sciences and mathematics, people usually have their greatest achievements during their younger ages, while this is the opposite for the more artistic fields.

Great scientists often fall to the distorted habit of only working on great problems. They start to ignore the small problems - the kind of problems that were foundational for building up their research and breakthroughs. And it is only through the small problems that the groundwork for the great problems can appear.

Perhaps peculiarly enough, people are often most productive when the working conditions are bad.

Another observation is that top scientists often have a tremendous drive to work, learn, and research.

Knowledge and productivity are like compound interest. Even if you are slightly more productive than the person next to you, as long as you work this way consistently, you will far surpass this person. The more you know, the more you learn; the more you learn, the more you can do; the more you can do, the more the opportunity. Although you must be careful with this because we have limited amount of time, so once you cross a certain threshold, you'll end up neglecting other parts of your life. You have to question yourself and decide whether this sacrifice is worth it or not. Historically, a lot of the top scientists are also the ones who obsessed over their work and ended up neglecting other affairs of life, such as their family. To some, this is worthwhile, to others, this is abhorrent behavior. You must choose for yourself. But the drive has to be intelligently applied. A misapplied drive won't get you very far.

Ambiguity is another common factor among great scientists. They don't treat theories as either true or false. They allow themselves to allow certain parts to be true, while being skeptical or doubtful of other parts. This in turn allows them to bring about new paradigms and improve their own theories. This requires a balance too, for too much acceptance and/or too much doubt can both be harmful. In particular, it is the things that break your assumptions/theories that can force you to analyze the faults of your theory and work to improve it, which can lead to a great contribution.

Emotional commitment is another necessary, but not sufficient condition that one has to have for their work.

To create important work, one must work on important problems. And to do that, one first needs to identify the important problems. One must also have a reasonable attack to approach and attempt to solve these problems. Without it, one will end up in a deadend.

When a great scientist discovers an opportunity for an important problem and have a reasonable attack, they can and oftentimes do drop all other things and go full in on this new opportunity to see how far they can take and whether they can solve it. In this sense, sitting on such opportunities instead can lead to these opportunities being seized by others.

Having a door to your office closed can make you end up be more productive, but the various interruptions you get from having your door open can lead to new interesting ideas, collaborations, inspirations, news about the field, and just general information about science. Hamming did observe a positive correlation between open doors and doing important things that was higher than that of having your door closed.

Sometimes, discovering a new way to solve an existing problem can also become a great contribution.

It is important to build the groundwork for others so that they can "stand on the shoulders of giants" and do important work as well. After all, science is both cumulative and collaborative. Thus, focus on making your work more easily accessible to others, not just in terms of the experiments, but reports, papers, speeches, everything. Don't make it so that others have to waste their time unnecessarily duplicating your results if you can provide the foundation for them in the first place.

It's not enough to just do the work, you must also sell it. The 3 primary things you need to do to sell are 1) write clearly and well; 2) be able to give reasonably formal talks; 3) be able to give informal talks. In particular, try not to be overly technical to make it easier for others to understand your work. Make your presentation a story that they can follow and understand the motivations for your work and how it is beneficial.

It may be hard, but you can educate and influence your bosses. You have to sell your ideas to them also.

The struggle and the journey are where most of the value in important research lies according to the author. Great success, fame, reputation, prestige, and all of those are dividends that come about because of the struggle.

Since one is always confined to the system, one must also be able to use the system to one's advantage whenever possible. Use the resources that the system offers because with it, one may be able to go farther than by themselves or while struggling against the system. But sometimes you do indeed have to push and sometimes you have to push hard and all the way. Half-hearted effort will not be enough. But this does come to inhibit your work as you have to spend the time and effort battling the system. So the question you must ask yourself then is, "Do I want to change the system or do first-class work?" Hamming advises to let others change the system, while you carry on doing first-class work, for it is very rare to be able to do both.

Being original is another helpful property, and whenever one is original in one field, chances are they are likely to be original in others as well.

Don't let negative emotions like anger get to you. Always try to focus on the positives rather than the negatives, and when faced with the negatives, try to change your perspective as to change those into positives.

Most of all, one must know themselves - their faults, weaknesses, strengths, what makes them tick, how to convert their faults into assets, how to use one's powers fully, and so on.

In such a pursuit of great science, stress is inevitable and can be quite significant. So once again, one must choose between a simpler life or a life of a great scientist.

Rather than brainstorming daily by yourself, Hamming recommends conversing about ideas and potential directions with other researchers instead. But you have to make sure you talk to the capable researchers who are not afraid to shut your idea down.

Reading is useful to understand what is going and what is possible. But reading too much and especially reading the solutions puts your mind and thinking into the framework of other people, of old theories, which may inhibit you from diverging from them and producing your own original thoughts, paths, and such. It's better to read just the basics, and then try to formulate the problem yourself, develop your own perspective, and try to come up with your own solution.

One way to combat the problems of fame and refusing to work on small problems just to work on the great problems is to switch your (sub)fields entirely and start over within that (sub)field from scratch. Then you will have to build up your way just like you originally did. Originality, after all, may not last forever within one field. Eventually, you'll use it up, so a solution is to shift to another field and start using your originality within this brand new scope. This is advice for everyone really, not just for great scientists. One should occassionally switch subfields to experience new perspectives, let go of their old ones, and come up with new and interesting research. That doesn't mean that one has to switch subfields forever. You may return to your original subfield down the line, but you should take a break from it for at least 7 years according to Hamming. And this doesn't mean just no longer doing research in that subfield. It also means no longer reading the latest papers in that subfield, thinking about it, engaging it. Try to distance yourself as much as possible for the next several years and completely commit to the new subfield.

Surround yourself with other great scientists and regularly interact with them.
