# Definition
> [!info] Social Comparison Theory (Festinger, 1954)[^1]
> People evaluate their abilities and opinions by comparing themselves with others, especially when objective standards are lacking. *Upward* comparison (with those better off) can inspire or demoralise; *downward* comparison (with those worse off) can comfort. The question is not whether we compare but by what standard.

# Properties
- **Exposure to extremes.** Attention is limited, so the information that reaches us is selected from the tails of the distribution: the best and the worst. A feed of the exceptional makes exceptionalism look normal and "average" look like failure, although most people are average at most things. If everyone were extraordinary, no one would be.[^2] This is a selection effect on the comparison sample (cf. [[Survivorship Bias]]).
- **Evidence on social media.** Frequent social-media use is associated with more upward comparison and slightly lower self-esteem (Vogel et al., 2014). Across large samples, the association between adolescents' digital technology use and well-being is negative but very small ($r \approx -0.04$ to $-0.1$) (Orben and Przybylski, 2019). Manson's claim that technology has "open-sourced insecurity" overstates the effect size.
- **Coping by extremes.** If average counts as failure, some people seek attention at the bottom end instead, as the most miserable or most victimised ([[Psychological Entitlement]]).[^3]
- Accepting that most of one's life will be ordinary removes the pressure to prove oneself, and frees one to enjoy basic experiences.[^4]
- Related: [[Status Motive]], [[Contingencies of Self-Worth]].
