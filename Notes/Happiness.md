# Definition

...

Happiness is active and fleeting. Contentment is passive.

Happiness can come from overcoming challenges.

Happiness is a result of our inner thoughts. Two people with the same physical conditions can be happy and unhappy depending on their mental point of view. Therefore, foster a positive mindset to get a continual state of contentment.

One way of becoming happy is by acting happy, as the action will increase the likelihood of the feeling coming.

# Factors
- Higher Age
- Social relationships
- Material Success only until the point that basic physical needs are provided for for low emotional well-being people. For high emotional well-being, higher income continues to increase happiness.
	- Reminder of wealth results in less enjoyment of simple experiences
- Religion in nations with difficult living conditions
- Possessing characteristics that are highly valued by one's culture
- Education (weak correlation)

# Non-Factors
- Intelligence
- Parenthood (some findings show that those without children are happier)
- Objective physical attractiveness (one's perceived level of physical attractiveness seems to predict happiness)

# Evidence
- Acting happy has a small real effect on mood ([[Facial Feedback Hypothesis]]).
- Counting blessings has small effects ([[Gratitude Intervention]]). So does helping others ([[Prosocial Behavior]]).
- Pursuing happiness directly can backfire ([[Backwards Law]]), and gains fade through [[Hedonic Adaptation]].
- **Correction: poor countries are not the happiest.** Burkeman says surveys find some of the poorest countries among the happiest, and that the least privileged are the most optimistic.[^antidote] Life evaluations rise with log income across and within countries (Deaton, 2008; Stevenson and Wolfers, 2013), and the poorest countries rank near the bottom of the World Happiness Report. Optimism and positive emotions are less tied to income, and surveyed rates of anxiety and depressive disorders are lower in low-income countries, though under-detection may explain part of that (Kessler et al.; Bromet et al., 2011). The plausible kernel is that happiness depends heavily on social relationships.
