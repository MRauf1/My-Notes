# Definition
> [!info] Psychological Entitlement[^1]
> A stable, pervasive sense that one deserves more and is owed more than others (Campbell et al., 2004). Entitled people construe every event as either an affirmation of, or a threat to, their own specialness, so the belief closes in on itself and resists correction.

# Types
- **Grandiose:** "I'm awesome and the rest of you suck, so I deserve special treatment."[^2]
- **Vulnerable:** "I suck and the rest of you are awesome, so I deserve special treatment." Constantly casting oneself as victimised takes as much self-focus as the grandiose form, and people can flip between the two.[^2] This matches the distinction between grandiose and vulnerable narcissism, which share an entitled, antagonistic core (Miller et al., 2011; Krizan and Herlache, 2018).

# Properties
- **Evidence.** Entitlement predicts selfish choices in resource dilemmas, aggression after criticism, and lower relationship quality (Campbell et al., 2004). It also predicts chronic disappointment and lower well-being, because unmet expectations are read as unfair (Grubbs and Exline, 2016).
- **Origin claimed by Manson.** Problems that feel unsolvable (e.g. trauma) lead people to conclude they are uniquely special or uniquely defective. Vulnerable narcissism is in fact associated with childhood adversity and insecure attachment, though the direction of causation is unclear.[^2]
- **Antidote.** There are no truly personal problems; millions share yours. Realising you and your problems are not special is the first step to solving them.[^3]
- Entitlement is not self-esteem: feeling good about oneself without reason doesn't help (see [[Self-Esteem]]); people who become exceptional are usually driven by a belief that they are *not* that great yet.[^4]
