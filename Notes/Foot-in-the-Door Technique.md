# Definition
> [!info] Foot-in-the-Door Technique[^1]
> Agreement to a small request increases compliance with a larger later request (Freedman and Fraser, 1966). One explanation is self-perception: having agreed, people see themselves as the kind of person who helps. A related explanation is the desire to stay consistent. Carnegie's Principle 5, "Get the other person saying 'yes, yes' immediately," appeals to the same consistency: "Once having said a thing, you feel you must stick to it."

# Properties
- **Evidence.**
	- Homeowners who first agreed to display a small safety sign were far more likely to accept a large, ugly billboard later (Freedman and Fraser, 1966).
	- Meta-analyses find the effect is reliable but *small*, and it disappears under some conditions (Burger, 1999).
	- Commitment and consistency is one of Cialdini's six principles of influence.
- **What Carnegie gets wrong.**
	- Overstreet's claim that saying "No" makes "the entire organism—glandular, nervous, muscular" gather into rejection has no physiological evidence.[^2]
	- Carnegie's version of the Socratic method is a stretch.[^3] Socrates' questioning (*elenchus*) usually led interlocutors into contradiction and perplexity (*aporia*), not into agreement.
- **Common ground.** Carnegie advises beginning with points of agreement and stressing that you share the same end and differ only in method.[^4] Emphasizing shared identity and goals improves cooperation and negotiation outcomes. Woodrow Wilson: "Let us sit down and take counsel together."
- **Begin in a friendly way.** People are more persuaded by sources they like (Cialdini's *liking* principle). "A drop of honey catches more flies than a gallon of gall."[^5]
