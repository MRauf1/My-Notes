# Definition
> [!info] Biological Naturalism[^1]
> Searle's view that [[Intentionality|intentionality]] is a biological phenomenon produced by the specific causal powers of the brain, so that only machines with causal powers equivalent to those of brains can think. It rests on two premises and three consequences:
> $$\begin{align}
> &(1)\ \text{Intentionality in humans and animals is a product of causal features of the brain.}\\
> &(2)\ \text{Instantiating a computer program is never by itself sufficient for intentionality.}\\
> &(3)\ \text{The brain does not produce intentionality by instantiating a program.} && (1,2)\\
> &(4)\ \text{Any mechanism producing intentionality must have causal powers equal to the brain's.} && (1)\\
> &(5)\ \text{Strong AI cannot succeed by programs alone; it must duplicate the brain's causal powers.} && (2,4)
> \end{align}$$

# Properties
- Premise (1) is taken as an empirical fact; premise (2) is established by the [[Chinese Room Argument]].[^1]
- "Only a machine could think": brains are machines, and there is no reason in principle why a machine could not understand, provided it has the brain's causal powers rather than merely running a program. [[Strong Artificial Intelligence]] has little to say about thinking because it concerns programs, and programs are not machines.[^2]
- Intentionality is as causally dependent on its specific biochemistry as lactation or photosynthesis: no one supposes a computer simulation of lactation produces milk, yet many believe the analogous "miracle" for minds because of an abiding [[Dualism|dualism]] that treats the mind as formal and independent of material causes.[^3]
- For any program, something can instantiate it without having mental states, so the brain's causal capacity for intentionality cannot consist in running a program.[^4]
- A position on the [[Mind-Body Problem]] that rejects both dualism and program-based functionalism.
