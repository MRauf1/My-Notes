# Definition
> [!info] Decatastrophizing[^1]
> A [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy|CBT]] technique that answers a feared outcome directly instead of avoiding it. You ask what the worst realistic outcome is, how likely it is, and how you would cope with it. Carnegie's version is Willis Carrier's "magic formula":
> 1. Ask "What is the worst that can possibly happen?"
> 2. Prepare to accept it if you have to.
> 3. Then calmly proceed to improve on the worst.

# Properties
- **Mechanism.** Carrier reported that once he accepted the worst, "I immediately relaxed… From that time on, I was able to think." Worry is partly a way of avoiding vivid images of the feared outcome. By keeping the threat abstract it prevents emotional processing (Borkovec's avoidance theory of worry). Facing the worst case concretely allows that processing.[^2]
- **Evidence.**
	- Decatastrophizing is a standard component of cognitive therapy for anxiety, which is well supported, but its effect *in isolation* has rarely been tested.
	- The closest tested relative is *worry exposure*: deliberately imagining the worst-case scenario in detail. In an RCT for [[Generalized Anxiety Disorder|GAD]], it was about as effective as applied relaxation (Hoyer et al., 2009).
- **Probability estimation.** Carnegie advises asking "by the law of averages" how likely the feared event is.
	- Studies of anxious people show strong overestimation of threat probability.
	- In people with GAD who kept a worry-outcome journal, 91.4% of worries did not come true. Seeing that evidence predicted greater improvement in symptoms (LaFreniere and Newman, 2020). Carnegie's "99%" is a rhetorical exaggeration of the same pattern.[^3]
- **Acceptance step.** "Be willing to have it so… acceptance of what has happened is the first step in overcoming the consequences of any misfortune" (William James).[^4] Compare [[Dichotomy of Control]] and the Stoic *premeditatio malorum* ([[Stoic Exercises]]).
- Related: [[Worry]], [[Exposure Therapy]], [[Problem-Solving Therapy]].
