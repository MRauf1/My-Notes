# Definition
> [!info] Second-Order Change[^1]
> A change in the frame or system itself rather than an incremental adjustment within it, which is a first-order change (Watzlawick, Weakland and Fisch, 1974). Burkeman, following the psychologist Stephanie Brown, applies it to "speed addiction". We hurry to quell anxiety about not keeping up, which only generates more anxiety. The way out is to surrender to the fact that things take the time they take, and then the anxiety is transformed.

# Properties
- **Speed as self-medication.** "As soon as I slow down, the feeling of anxiety wells up inside, and I look for something to take it away." Unlike other addictions, speed addiction is socially celebrated.[^2]
- **Surrender.** The model is Alcoholics Anonymous: recovery begins when the drinker gives up the impossible attempt at control.[^3] A Cochrane review found AA and twelve-step facilitation at least as effective as other established treatments for sustained abstinence (Kelly et al., 2020). "Speed addiction" itself is a metaphor, not a clinical construct.
- **Patience in practice.** Making yourself look at a single painting for three hours, the discomfort of slow time eventually gives way, and the real experience begins.[^4]
- **Problems resolve.** If you endure the discomfort of not knowing, a solution often presents itself, rather than racing to any resolution just to feel in control (M. Scott Peck).[^5]
