# Definition
> [!info] Turing Test[^1]
> A thought experiment proposed by Alan Turing (1950) to sidestep the question "Can a machine think?" A computer passes the test if a human interrogator, after posing some written questions, cannot tell whether the written responses come from a person or from the computer.

# Properties
- Passing the test requires natural language processing to communicate in a human language, knowledge representation to store what is known or heard, automated reasoning to answer questions and draw new conclusions, and [[Machine Learning|machine learning]] to adapt to new circumstances and detect/extrapolate patterns.
- All of the skills required to pass the Turing Test also suffice for an agent to act rationally, connecting it to the notion of a [[Rational Agent]].
- Extended by the [[Total Turing Test]], which additionally requires interaction with the physical world.
- Introduced in Turing's paper [[Computing Machinery and Intelligence (Turing)]].
- Originally formulated as the imitation game: a man (A), a woman (B), and an interrogator (C) in a separate room; C must determine which is the man and which the woman, A tries to cause a wrong identification, and B tries to help C. The test then asks what happens when a machine takes the part of A.[^2]
- The question-and-answer format, with C unable to see, touch, or hear the other players, deliberately makes physical attributes irrelevant, while allowing almost any field of human endeavour to be probed.[^3]
- Only digital computers are permitted as the machine, and the question is not whether all or currently available computers would do well, but whether there are *imaginable* computers that would.[^4]
- The game may be criticised as weighted too heavily against the machine, since a machine might think in ways very different from a human; but if a machine nevertheless plays satisfactorily, this objection need not trouble us.[^5]
- Its adequacy as a test of understanding is challenged by the [[Chinese Room Argument]]: two systems may both pass the test while only one understands.
- Turing addresses the [[Objections to Machine Intelligence]] to the machine's ability to pass the test.
