# Review[^1]

1) Don't wait to write. Write as you are doing research; don't wait until the finish line. Writing is a primary mechanism for doing research, not just for reporting it.
	1) Forces you to be clear, focused.
	2) Shows you what we don't understand.
	3) Allows you to immediately talk to others about your research.
2) Identify your key idea. 
	1) Infect the mind of the reader with your idea. 
	2) You do not need to have a fantastic idea before you start research. Writing the paper is how you develop the idea in the first place. 
	3) A paper should have a single clear and sharp idea that you should already know and understand by the time you finish writing the paper (might not know in the beginning). Make sure you properly convey this idea to the reader. Thus, each idea should be its own paper.
3) Tell a story.
4) Nail your contributions to the mast. 
	1) Within the introduction, properly describe the problem and state your contributions in a single page. 
	2) You can use an example to introduce the problem. This can be more enticing to the reader than talking about something more general. Again, make sure to capture the reader's interest and attention. 
	3) You can present your contributions in a bulleted list format. 
	4) Contributions should be refutable. Thus, your introduction makes the claims, and it is the body of your paper that provides the evidence/proof for your claims. 
	5) When finishing your paper, go to each contribution listed in the introduction, find the evidence/proof in the body of the research paper for this contribution, and forward-reference it from the claim. Use forward-references from the narrative in the introduction rather than "This paper is structured in the following sections: ..." Introduction should act as a survey for the paper and thus should have forward-references to every important part of the paper.
5) Related work: later. (A bit of an uncommon approach)
	1) Reader knows nothing about the problem yet, so the summary of related work might be incomprehensible to them.
	2) Describing alternative approaches gets between the reader and your idea.
	3) It is a fallacy that to make your idea look good you have to make other people's work look bad. Be generous to the competition. Acknowledge weaknesses in your own approach. Giving credit to others does not diminish the credit you get from your own paper.
6) Put your readers first.
	1) Rather than the technical details, convey the intuition first as though you were describing your paper to someone in front of a whiteboard. Once the reader has the intuition, they can then follow the more technical details more easily.
	2) Introduce the problem and your idea using examples first, before presenting the general case.
7) Listen to your readers.
	1) Get your paper read by as many people as possible regardless of their qualifications. Every input can be helpful.
	2) Can send the paper to your competition for them to help you present their work fairly.
	3) Be equally grateful for both praise and criticism. Read every criticism as a positive suggestion for something you can improve upon. Upon criticism, fix your paper so that X is apparent even to the stupidest reader. Thank your reviewers warmly as they have given their time for you.
8) Language/Style.
	1) Use active voice.
	2) Use simple, direct language.
