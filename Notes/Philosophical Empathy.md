# Definition
> [!info] Philosophical Empathy[^1]
> The Socratic–Stoic remedy for anger, resentment and vanity. One understands oneself and others as fallible, and sees others' wrongdoing as the product of ignorance and of a causal history ("no one does evil knowingly"). This removes anger and blame while still allowing calm action to prevent harm and to re-educate the wrongdoer.

# Properties
- **From determinism.** Contemplating that human actions are caused by a complex network of prior factors ([[Stoic Determinism]]) leads to understanding and forgiveness, and to reforming rather than punishing wrongdoers. Socrates' sage wishes to do good even to enemies, meaning to educate them. Dubois: take people "as they are", admitting "they are never otherwise than what they can be"; first feel the "noble rage", then calm down, see "the threads which pull the human puppets", and act usefully. Wolpe likewise grounded therapist objectivity and empathy in causal determinism, using it to counteract clients' neurotic self-blame.[^1]
- **Against labelling.** Epictetus: don't call others "damn idiots"; try to understand the reasons for their errors (*Discourses* 1.18). Judging others to be nothing but idiots only shows our own ignorance of their motives.[^2]
- **Balance.** Understand others' distress and respond appropriately, but hold back internally from sharing their errors of judgement, as with a child "frightened of shadows": the fear is based on a false perception, but the emotion is real.[^3]
- **Inoculation against insult.** By trying to understand a critic, the Stoic either finds the criticism valid and can act on it, or sees it as misconception or bias and stops being disturbed. Socrates welcomed ridicule from the comic poets: if they said anything worth hearing he would change, otherwise it didn't apply to him. Epictetus: a critic obviously doesn't know me, or he'd have mentioned my other faults too.[^4]
- **Pragmatic justification.** Even if one couldn't decide whether wrongdoers act from malice or ignorance, the Socratic view ([[Socratic Intellectualism]]) is preferable, because blaming brings no advantage and feeds unhealthy emotions.[^5]
- **Anger and vanity share a root:** overvaluing others' opinions and actions. Others' views are their own business and outside our control. Blaming oneself is equally a mistake: present judgements, not past actions, cause our disturbance, and all blame overvalues its object and undervalues the act of judging ([[Cognitive Mediation]]).[^6]
- **Marcus's remedies for anger** (*Meditations* 11.18), condensed:[^7]
	1. Remember the bond between yourself and the rest of mankind ([[Cosmopolitanism]]).
	2. Consider the offenders' characters and how their thinking drives them.
	3. If they act rightly there is no complaint; if wrongly, it is involuntary, since no one errs deliberately.
	4. You are no different and have the same inclinations.
	5. You can't be sure they are doing wrong; motives are often hidden.
	6. Life is brief; soon we'll all be laid to rest.
	7. It's not their acts that upset us but the colour we put on them; you haven't really been harmed.
	8. Anger hurts us more than what we're angry about.
	9. Genuine kindness is irresistible; correct gently and privately, without sarcasm.
	10. Expecting bad people never to do bad things is madness, and expecting them to spare only you is arrogance.
	
	Avoid the opposite extreme of toadying. Calm gentleness shows more strength than rage.
- **Ellis's parallel rules (REBT):** don't blame others, since misdeeds come from stupidity, ignorance or disturbance. When blamed, first check whether you did wrong, and if not, see the criticism as the critic's problem. Try to stop wrongdoing calmly, otherwise accept it philosophically ("too bad, but not catastrophic"). Never blame yourself either ([[Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy]]).[^8]
- **Evidence.** Experimentally weakening belief in free will (e.g. by exposure to neuroscience) reduced retributive punishment while leaving consequentialist punishment intact (Shariff et al., 2014). This is consistent with the claim that a mechanistic view of behaviour turns blame toward reform.
- In Robertson's regimen: "Love mankind, and wish your enemies to become so happy and enlightened that they cease to be your enemies."[^9]
