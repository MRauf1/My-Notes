# Definition
> [!info] Nicotine Withdrawal[^1]
> The syndrome that follows stopping nicotine: craving, irritability or anger, anxiety, difficulty concentrating, restlessness, low mood, increased appetite, and insomnia. Most symptoms peak in the first week and fade over 2–4 weeks (Hughes, 2007).

# Properties
- **Carr's claim.** The physical withdrawal is extremely mild ("1 per cent physical and 99 per cent mental"). The "uptight, anxious, churned-up, angry feeling" people call withdrawal is a real physical response to a mental process: "I want a cigarette... I can't have one... AAARGH!"[^2]
- **Evidence against the 99% figure.** Withdrawal symptoms are documented in controlled studies, and nicotine replacement reduces them. That is part of why nicotine replacement raises quit rates.
- **Evidence for the mental component.** Craving tracks *perceived availability* more than time since the last cigarette. In flight attendants, craving depended on how soon they expected to be able to smoke, not on how long the flight had been (Dar et al., 2010). Carr makes the same point: you don't need the drug to relieve withdrawal, only to know you're about to get it, and "when you know you can't have something, it's easy to put it out of your mind."[^3]
- **Reacting to the thought.** "It isn't the thought that's important; it's how you react to the thought that matters." Cognitive reappraisal of craving cues does measurably reduce craving (Kober et al., 2010).[^4]
- Carr's claim that no transitional period is needed and that "the craving goes whenever you choose" is not supported by evidence.[^5]
