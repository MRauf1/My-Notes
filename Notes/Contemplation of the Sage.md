# Definition
> [!info] Contemplation of the Sage[^1]
> One of the most fundamental techniques of ancient philosophical therapy: discussing in public, and contemplating and visualising in private, the ideal [[Stoic Sage|sage]] and his virtues. The sage is the imaginary embodiment, role model and ultimate goal of philosophical practice. [[Epictetus]] argued that we need a single standard against which to measure our actions; without it we act at random or by an irrational standard (*Discourses* 3.23.1).

# Types
The therapeutic uses of contemplating the sage:[^2]
1. The image of the sage provides a general goal to aim at.
2. Ask what the sage would do when faced with a specific challenge.
3. Imagine being observed by a sage and how one would act in their presence.
4. Imagine the sage challenging, or proposing alternatives to, one's problem thoughts and actions.
5. Verbally describe or write down the virtues of one's peers and heroes, which makes them easier to remember.

# Properties
- **Role models.** Epictetus repeatedly names [[Socrates]] and Diogenes the Cynic ([[Cynicism]]), sometimes Heraclitus. Marcus contrasts the same three with Alexander, Caesar and Pompey: "Their minds were their own. The others? Nothing but anxiety and enslavement." Seneca also accepted Cato the Younger. The sage ideal was also linked with gods and their mortal incarnations.[^3]
- **"What would the sage do?"** In CBT it is common to ask whether someone else would necessarily share one's perspective. Epictetus follows his famous remark about judgements (*Enchiridion* 5) with the point that even death isn't a catastrophe in everyone's eyes. That others, especially the wise, see events differently is a reminder that there is always an alternative perspective, i.e. another of the "two handles" (*Enchiridion* 43).[^4] Plutarch: reflect on famous men who were unaffected by circumstances like one's own. When feverish, seeing others enjoy the same food makes us blame the illness, not the food.[^5]
- **Internal critic.** The sage acts as a rational conscience, more like Socrates' *daemon* than a theological moral authority, so the self-criticism is therapeutic rather than moralising.[^6]
- **Personal example.** "The road is a long one if one proceeds by way of precepts but short and effectual if by way of personal example" (Seneca).[^7]
- **Modelling.** Book 1 of Marcus's *Meditations* is a deliberate modelling exercise. He reviews the significant people in his life and condenses each one's virtues into a few words so he can memorise and imitate them, even though they are imperfect. In behaviour therapy, modelling is learning by observation: the therapist, or another role model, demonstrates behaviour the client imitates ([[Observational Learning]]).[^8]
- **Caveat.** Commitment to an ideal of perfection can turn into perfectionism. Socrates' disavowal of wisdom relieves this burden ([[Socratic Irony]]).[^9]
- Part of the morning routine ([[Prospective and Retrospective Meditation]]): "Ask yourself, 'What would someone with absolute wisdom do today?'"[^10]
