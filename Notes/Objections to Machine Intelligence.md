# Definition
> [!info] Objections to Machine Intelligence[^1]
> The family of arguments, catalogued and rebutted by Alan Turing (1950), against the claim that a digital computer could ever succeed in the imitation game ([[Turing Test]]) and hence be said to think.

# Types
- The Theological Objection - thinking is a function of an immortal soul given only to humans.
- [[Heads in the Sand Objection]] - the consequences of machines thinking would be too dreadful, so we hope they cannot.
- The Mathematical Objection - limitative results such as Gödel's incompleteness theorem show there are questions a discrete-state machine cannot answer.
- [[Argument from Consciousness]] - a machine cannot think unless it feels and is aware of its own thinking.
- [[Argument from Various Disabilities]] - a machine can never do some particular thing X (be kind, fall in love, learn from experience, ...).
- [[Lady Lovelace's Objection]] - a machine can only do what we tell it to do.
- [[Argument from Continuity in the Nervous System]] - the nervous system is not a discrete-state machine.
- The Argument from Informality of Behaviour - human conduct cannot be captured by a finite set of rules.
- The Argument from Extrasensory Perception - telepathy and similar phenomena would give humans an advantage in the game.

# Properties
- Turing's general strategy is to replace the ill-defined question "Can machines think?" with the operational question of performance in the imitation game, and then show that each objection either fails, begs the question, or applies equally to humans.
- Turing's constructive answer to the objections is the [[Child Machine]]: a learning machine educated towards adult intelligence.
