# Definition
> [!info] Active Listening[^1]
> Listening with full attention and trying to understand the speaker's meaning from *their* frame of reference, and showing that understanding through paraphrase, questions and nonjudgmental responses (Rogers and Farson, 1957). Carnegie's Principle 4: "Be a good listener. Encourage others to talk about themselves."

# Properties
- **Evidence.**
	- Speakers who received high-quality listening felt less socially anxious. They became more self-aware of their views and held less extreme, more nuanced attitudes (Itzchakov et al., 2017).
	- People who felt heard by their supervisors reported less emotional exhaustion and more trust.
	- In a laboratory test, active listening made speakers feel more *understood* than simple acknowledgments did, though not always more satisfied with the conversation (Weger et al., 2014).
- **Listening defuses complaints.** "The chronic kicker… will frequently soften… in the presence of a patient, sympathetic listener." Lincoln wanted not advice but "a friendly, sympathetic listener."[^2] Feeling heard reduces defensiveness, the precondition for persuasion (see [[Motivational Interviewing]]).
- **Understanding first.** Carl Rogers: "Our first reaction to most of the statements… is an evaluation or judgment, rather than an understanding of it." When disagreeing, Carnegie advises:
	- Listen first, without interrupting.
	- Look for areas of agreement.
	- Postpone action so both sides can think.[^3]
- **Listening as status.** Undivided attention signals that the speaker matters ("few human beings are proof against the implied flattery of rapt attention").[^4] Compare [[Status Motive]].
