# Definition
> [!info] Epicurean Argument Against Fearing Death[^1]
> (Epicurus) "Death is nothing to us, since when we are, death has not come, and when death has come, we are not." Death ends the experiencing subject, and with it any capacity to experience the state we fear; so if life doesn't continue beyond death, there is nothing in death to be feared.

# Types
- **No-subject argument.** As stated above.[^1]
- **Symmetry argument** (Lucretius, *De Rerum Natura* III; Burkeman attributes it to Epicurus). We don't look back with horror at the eternity of non-existence before birth, which was just as eternal and just as much oblivion as the one after death; so we should not fear the latter. Nabokov: "our existence is but a brief crack of light between two eternities of darkness", yet we view the pre-natal abyss far more calmly.[^2]

# Properties
- **Freud's objection.** The fear comes not from imagining death wrongly but from being unable to imagine it at all. Nagel's reply: unimaginability isn't terrifying in itself, since we can't imagine dreamless sleep yet surrender to it nightly; "people who are averse to death are not usually averse to unconsciousness."[^3]
- **Logic doesn't remove fear.** Showing that a fear is illogical rarely makes it go away (Tillinghast).[^3]
- Even if death is not to be *feared*, it can still be *bad* because of what it ends ([[Deprivation Account of Death]]). Understanding death as not fearsome but still bad may be the ideal middle path: it allows contemplating death ([[Memento Mori]]) without devaluing life.[^4]
