# Definition
> [!info] Worry Postponement (Stimulus Control for Worry)[^1]
> A technique from Borkovec et al. (1983):
> 1. Notice worry as it starts.
> 2. Postpone it to a fixed, daily 30-minute "worry period" in a fixed place.
> 3. Return attention to the present task.
> 4. Use the worry period to worry or problem-solve.
>
> One of Carnegie's contributors used a looser version: "never to fret over a problem until it is at least a week old", because time often dissolves it.

# Properties
- **Evidence.**
	- In the original study, stimulus control reduced worry time compared with controls (Borkovec et al., 1983).
	- An RCT found that postponement reduced worry frequency and intolerance of uncertainty, but did not outperform self-monitoring alone on all outcomes (McGowan and Behar, 2013).
	- It is used as one component of CBT for [[Generalized Anxiety Disorder|GAD]].
- **Why it helps.** Worry feels uncontrollable. Postponing it shows that it can be controlled. Many worries also lose their urgency by the scheduled time ("the worry… will often collapse like a pricked balloon").[^2]
- **Taking troubles one at a time.** Carnegie's hourglass image: take tasks and troubles one at a time rather than all at once.[^3] Compare [[Work-in-Progress Limit]].
