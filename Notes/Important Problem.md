# Definition
> [!info] Important Problem[^1]
> (Hamming) A research problem is important not because of its consequences but because one has a *reasonable attack* on it. Time travel, teleportation, and antigravity are not important problems in this sense, since no one has an attack.

# Properties
- "If you do not work on an important problem, it's unlikely you'll do important work"; most scientists fail to ask "What are the important problems in my field?"[^2]
- Great scientists keep a list of important problems in mind; when an opportunity opens, their prepared minds drop other things and pursue it. Most attempts fail, but few hits are needed, and "one of the chief tricks is to live a long time."[^3]
- Luck favours the prepared mind: which particular thing one does is luck, but that one does something important is not.[^4]
- Choosing well also means solving problems as instances of a class rather than in isolation, so that others can build on the work: by changing a problem slightly one can do great rather than merely good work, and a general job is as easy as a special case.[^5]
- Compare the *interest* axis of the [[Feasibility-Interest Diagram]] (a reasonable attack corresponds to feasibility); see also [[Research Heuristics]] and [[You and Your Research (Hamming)]].
