# Definition
> [!info] Premeditatio Malorum[^1]
> ("Premeditation of evils"; philosophical premeditation) The Stoic and broadly Hellenistic exercise of preparing the mind for adversity. One imagines future misfortunes (poverty, exile, illness, death) as if they were already happening, then restores calm by rehearsing rational precepts (*epilogismoi*) showing that they are not true evils, since they don't depend on us.

# Properties
- **Mechanism (Philo).** Foresight lessens even the most painful events: when nothing is unexpected, thought "dulls the perception of them, as if they were old, worn-out things".[^1]
- **Foucault's three features.**[^2]
	1. Imagine the *worst* that can happen, "the worst as certainty", not the probable outcome.
	2. Imagine it as already actual and taking place, not as a distant possibility.
	3. Do this not to suffer but to convince oneself that these are not real ills, only things we have to accept.
- **Precepts as weapons.** The precepts rehearsed are "dogmas", maxims or formulas, kept "at hand" (*procheiron*) as the "weapons" of the Stoic. Spinoza: by often reflecting on likely wrongs together with the rule for meeting them, one associates the image of the wrong with the precept, so the precept is ready when the wrong occurs.[^3]
- **Meditatio vs gymnasia.** The Stoics divided exercises into *meditatio*, imaginary experience that trains thought, and *gymnasia*, training in real (even artificially induced) situations such as voluntary hardship. Seneca: "in time of peace, prepare for war".[^2]
- Usually done in the morning ([[Prospective and Retrospective Meditation]]). Its culmination is the meditation on death (*melete thanatou*), the ultimate premeditation of evils ([[Memento Mori]], [[Stoic View of Death]]). The aim is to love life yet not fear death: "a person who has learned how to meet his death has unlearned how to be a slave" (Seneca).[^4]
- Shared by other schools, e.g. the Epicurean "I have anticipated you, Fortune, and have barred your means of entry".[^5]
- Plato's reasons for calm in misfortune (*Republic* 604b–d): the outcome may turn out good or bad; taking it hard doesn't help; human affairs aren't worth great seriousness; grief obstructs the deliberation needed. "Accept what has happened as we would the fall of the dice", and then turn to the remedy.[^6]
- **Modern analogues.**[^7]
	- *Imaginal exposure / decatastrophizing* in Beck's cognitive therapy of anxiety: repeatedly visualise the feared catastrophe as if it were happening now. This tends to reduce anxiety on its own and to shift perspective, challenging the anxious belief "Something bad is going to happen that I won't be able to handle". See [[Exposure Therapy]] and [[Decatastrophizing]].
	- *Imaginal problem-solving rehearsal*: imagine a desperate situation, let the usual despair arise, then forcefully rehearse generating solutions anyway ([[Problem-Solving Therapy]]).
	- Russell's method: consider deliberately the very worst that could happen, give yourself sound reasons why it wouldn't be so terrible, and repeat until worry gives way to "a kind of exhilaration".
- **Versus worry.** Worry dwells on fears without resolving them. It is kept going by the metacognitive belief that dwelling on danger is useful, which Ellis listed as an irrational idea. Premeditation faces fears calmly and systematically, aiming at problem solving or acceptance ([[Worry]]).[^8]
- One of the [[Stoic Exercises]]; based on the [[Dichotomy of Control]].
