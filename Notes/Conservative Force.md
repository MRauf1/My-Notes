# Definition
> [!info] Conservative Force[^1]
> A [[Force]] for which the total [[Work]] done on an object moved around any closed path is zero; equivalently, the work done in moving between two points is independent of the path taken between them.

# Properties
- Path-independence can be shown by approximating any path with a fine grid of segments taken along and across level surfaces of the force, whose individual contributions are easy to compute, without changing the net work in the limit of arbitrarily fine subdivision.
- Path-independence of work is precisely what allows a [[Potential Energy]] to be defined as a function of position alone, rather than of the history of motion.
- The [[Gravitation|gravitational]] force is conservative; the [[Conservative Vector Field|analogous notion in vector calculus]] is a vector field that is the gradient of a scalar potential.
- If a force were not conservative, one could extract unlimited energy by repeatedly traversing a closed loop, in violation of [[Conservation of Energy]].
- All fundamental forces of nature appear to be conservative; apparently nonconservative forces, such as friction, are macroscopic averages over microscopic interactions that are themselves conservative, with the "lost" mechanical energy actually converted into heat (see [[Conservation of Mechanical Energy]]).

[^1]: [The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Vol. I, Ch. 13: Work and Potential Energy (A)](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_13.html)
