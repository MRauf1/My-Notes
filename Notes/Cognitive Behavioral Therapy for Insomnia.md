# Definition
> [!info] Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I)[^1]
> First-line treatment for chronic insomnia. It combines:
> - **Stimulus control**: get out of bed if you can't sleep, so the bed is associated with sleeping (Bootzin, 1972).
> - **Sleep restriction**: limit time in bed to consolidate sleep.
> - **Cognitive therapy** for sleep-related worry.
> - Relaxation and sleep hygiene.
>
> Carnegie's key claim matches its cognitive model: "Worrying about insomnia will hurt you far more than insomnia."

# Properties
- **Evidence.** A meta-analysis found that CBT-I improves sleep onset latency, wake time after sleep onset and sleep efficiency, with effects lasting beyond treatment (Trauer et al., 2015). The American College of Physicians recommends it as first-line treatment before medication (Qaseem et al., 2016).
- **Cognitive model.** Worry and monitoring about sleep raise arousal, which perpetuates insomnia (Harvey, 2002). People with insomnia underestimate how much they actually sleep ("I never slept a wink last night"). *Sleep effort*, trying hard to sleep, inhibits sleep (Espie et al., 2006). *Paradoxical intention*, trying to stay awake, is an evidence-supported component.[^2]
- **Stimulus control in practice.** Sam Untermyer got up and studied instead of lying awake worrying.[^3] This is essentially stimulus control.
- **Exercise.** Regular exercise modestly improves sleep quality (Kredlow et al., 2015). Carnegie's advice to make yourself "physically tired" is reasonable. "Physically exhausting" work is unnecessary.[^4]
- **Corrections.**
	- "No man ever committed suicide by refusing to sleep" is misleading. Total sleep deprivation is fatal in rats within weeks (Rechtschaffen and Bergmann, 1995). In humans, fatal familial insomnia kills. Chronic short sleep is associated with higher mortality.[^4]
	- Prayer as a "sleep-producing agent" is untested. Feelings of safety and lower pre-sleep arousal do help.[^5]
