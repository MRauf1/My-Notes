# Definition
> [!info] Pleasure-Pain System[^1]
> An [[Unorganized Machine|unorganized machine]] with two interfering inputs, one for "pleasure" or reward ($R$) and one for "pain" or punishment ($P$), whose configuration is described by a *character-expression* and a *situation-expression*: the character and situation at any moment, together with the input signals, determine the character and situation at the next moment. The character may be subject to random variation; pleasure stimuli tend to fix the character (prevent it changing), while pain stimuli tend to disrupt it, causing fixed features to change or become subject to random variation again.

# Properties
- Motivated by the training of the human child, which depends largely on a system of rewards and punishments; organising a machine into a universal machine is most impressive if the interference involves very few inputs, ideally just these two.[^1]
- A change of *character* is thought of as a change in the machine itself, whereas the *situation* is merely the configuration of the machine described by the character (roughly, learned parameters versus current state).[^2]
- Pain stimuli are applied when behaviour is wrong and pleasure stimuli when it is particularly right; with stimuli judiciously operated by a "teacher", the character should converge to the desired one, i.e. wrong behaviour becomes rare. This is the principle of [[Operant Conditioning]] and an early form of [[Reinforcement Learning]] with a [[Reward (Artificial Intelligence)|reward]] signal.[^2]
- Turing's particular P-type machine was organised by letting it wander at random through situations, applying pain when a wrong choice was made and pleasure when the right one was made. Pain should also be applied for irrelevant choices, to prevent the machine becoming isolated in a ring of irrelevant situations.[^3]
- Turing found this organising technique somewhat disappointing, being insufficiently analogous to how a child is actually taught.[^3]
- A P-type machine with randomly distributed memory units might, like humans with irregular verbs, pick up and apply complicated rules without being able to enunciate them; verifying this requires painstaking individual training, since "mass-production" methods such as built-in teaching procedures cannot help.[^4]
- Training produces only discipline; intelligence also requires initiative ([[Discipline and Initiative]]).
