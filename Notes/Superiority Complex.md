# Definition
> [!info] Superiority Complex[^1]
> A development of the [[Inferiority Complex]]. Unable to accept one's "incapable self", one takes an easy way out: acting as if one were superior and indulging in a fabricated feeling of superiority.

# Properties
- **Giving authority.** Making oneself look bigger with borrowed power, such as famous acquaintances or credentials. One then lives by other people's value systems, that is, lives other people's lives.[^2]
- **Boasting.** Adler: "The one who boasts does so only out of a feeling of inferiority." Someone with real confidence feels no need to boast.[^3]
- Self-affirmation, meaning telling oneself "I can do it" about what is beyond one's ability, can produce a superiority complex. Contrast [[Self-Acceptance]].[^4]
- "Being normal is not being incapable. One does not need to flaunt one's superiority." Wanting to be special comes from not accepting one's normal self. When being especially good fails, people jump to being especially bad.[^5]
- Related: [[Psychological Entitlement]], [[Specialness (Existential Psychology)]].
- **Messiah complex.** Trying to be saved oneself by saving others: passing oneself off as a saviour to realize one's own worth. It is a form of superiority complex in people who cannot dispel their feelings of inferiority.[^kk1]

# Evidence
- **Correction: the "mask model" has weak support.** The idea that grandiosity hides low self-esteem is not well supported for narcissism generally. Grandiose narcissists usually report high explicit self-esteem, and the implicit self-esteem evidence is inconsistent (Bosson et al., 2008). Fragile, contingent self-esteem is linked to defensive self-enhancement (Kernis, 2003), so the claim holds for some boasters but not as a universal law.
