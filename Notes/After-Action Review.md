# Definition
> [!info] After-Action Review (AAR)[^1]
> A structured debrief held after every mission or exercise, *regardless of outcome*: what was planned, what happened, why, and what to change. It is a "live autopsy". Goggins' version for failures:
> 1. Write out everything that went well.
> 2. Note how you handled the failure and whether it affected your life and relationships.
> 3. Examine how you were thinking during preparation and execution.
> 4. List what can be fixed, with brutal honesty.
> 5. Schedule another attempt as soon as possible.

# Properties
- **Evidence.**
	- A meta-analysis found that debriefs improve individual and team performance by about 25% on average ($d \approx 0.67$), with larger effects when they are facilitated and structured (Tannenbaum and Cerasoli, 2013).
	- Soldiers who debriefed both successes and failures learned more than those who debriefed failures only (Ellis and Davidi, 2005). This supports Goggins' choice to start with what went well.[^2]
- **No entitlement.** Don't focus on what you think you deserve. Blaming others or bad luck blocks the review.[^3]
- **Tactical review.** The final and most vital part: find the tactical advantage you were missing.[^4]
- Related: [[Progress Monitoring]], [[Deliberate Practice]], [[Habit]] (reflection and review).

- **Weekly self-review (Carnegie).** A banker reviewed each week's appointments, asking: what mistakes did I make, what did I do right and how could I have improved, and what lessons can I learn? Over the years his blunders became less frequent.[^5] This is the same structure as an AAR, applied to oneself. Darwin spent years criticising his own conclusions before publishing.[^6]
