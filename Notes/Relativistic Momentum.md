# Definition
> [!info] Relativistic Momentum
> $$\mathbf{p} = \gamma m \mathbf{v} = \frac{m\mathbf{v}}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}$$
> The momentum of a particle of rest [[Mass]] $m$ moving at velocity $\mathbf{v}$, where $\gamma$ is the Lorentz factor and $c$ is the speed of light. Reduces to the Newtonian $\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v}$ only when $v \ll c$.

# Properties
- Diverges to infinity as $v \to c$, which is why an infinite amount of energy would be required to accelerate a massive particle to the speed of light — this divergence in momentum (and total energy), not an actual growth of an object's mass, is the correct relativistic statement behind the informal claim that "mass becomes infinite" near $c$; modern usage instead treats [[Mass]] as an invariant, frame-independent rest mass.
- Is the spatial part of the [[Four-Momentum]], together with total energy $E$.
- For a massless particle such as a [[Photon]], the formula above is not applicable (it would give $0/0$ at $v=c$); instead momentum is given by $p = E/c$, consistent with the massless limit of the [[Relativistic Energy-Momentum Relation]].
- Frame-dependent: observers in relative motion measure different momenta for the same particle, even though the particle's invariant [[Mass]] is the same for every observer.
- Historically (and still in some older texts) expressed instead via a velocity-dependent "relativistic mass" $m(v) = \gamma m_0$, writing momentum in the Newtonian-looking form $p = m(v)\,v$; this is numerically identical to $p=\gamma m_0 v$ above, but modern usage avoids it, treating $m_0$ (written simply as [[Mass|$m$]]) as the particle's sole, invariant mass.
- [[Conservation of Momentum]] holds in special relativity using this quantity in place of the Newtonian $m\mathbf{v}$.
