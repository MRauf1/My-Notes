# Definition
> [!info] Choice Architecture[^1]
> The design of the context in which people choose. Changing how options are presented (placement, visibility, defaults, effort required) changes behavior without changing the options or the incentives (Thaler and Sunstein's "nudges"). "People often choose products not because of what they are, but because of where they are."

# Properties
- **Lewin's equation.** Behavior is a function of the person in their environment, $B = f(P, E)$ (Kurt Lewin, 1936). "Environment is the invisible hand that shapes human behavior."[^2]
- **Evidence.**[^3]
	- Anne Thorndike's redesign of a hospital cafeteria cut soda sales by 11.4% and raised bottled water sales by 25.8% over three months, without talking to anyone.
	- Defaults are the most robust nudge. A meta-analysis found a mean effect of $d \approx 0.68$ (Jachimowicz et al., 2019). Automatic escalation of retirement contributions raised savings rates from 3.5% to 13.6% (Save More Tomorrow; Thaler and Benartzi, 2004).
	- Nudge effects overall are heterogeneous. One meta-analysis estimated $d \approx 0.43$ (Mertens et al., 2022), but after correcting for publication bias there was little evidence of an average effect (Maier et al., 2022). The type of nudge matters.
- **Salience.** We are more likely to notice cues that stand out, so make the cues of good habits obvious and the cues of bad habits invisible. The most persistent behaviors usually have multiple cues.[^4]
- **Friction.** By the principle of least effort (Zipf, 1949), people choose the option that needs the least work among similar options. Reduce friction for good behaviors and increase it for bad ones. Prime the environment so the next action is easy ("addition by subtraction"). "It is remarkable how little friction is required to prevent unwanted behavior."[^5]
- **Automation.** One-time choices and technology lock in future behavior. This is especially useful for behaviors too infrequent to become habits. But near-zero friction also lets us act on every whim.[^6]
