# Definition
> [!info] Autosuggestion[^1]
> Influencing one's own feelings, beliefs and bodily states by repeatedly presenting ideas or images to oneself. Émile Coué's method (e.g. repeating "Day by day, in every way, I'm getting better and better") held that imagination evokes physical and emotional reactions more powerfully than intellect alone. So one must "fight fire with fire", using empowering images against enfeebling ones.

# Properties
- Coué's view resembles Spinoza's on the power of imagination ("the incredible power of mental representations").[^1]
- The Stoics also didn't rely solely on abstract reason. Their vivid imagery and rhetoric show they understood that imagination, guided by reason, must be turned on itself to produce real emotional change ([[View from Above]], [[Premeditatio Malorum]]).[^2]
- **Law of habit (Baudouin).** Exercises must be practised assiduously, daily if possible. This is common to yoga, the Stoics' exercise of the will, and autosuggestion ([[Stoic Exercises]], [[Habit]]).[^3]
- **Ellis's critique.** Couéism encourages positive thinking without disputing the original negative thoughts. "Accentuating the positive is itself a false system of belief"; such Pollyannaism "can be as pernicious as the negative claptrap" behind neurosis. REBT instead aims at "a thoroughgoing revealing and uprooting of the negative nonsense".[^4]
- **Ellis's reinterpretation.** Suggestion and autosuggestion can remove symptoms because they are the very instruments that produced them. Most sustained adult emotions come from ideas first suggested by others (parents, teachers, books) and then continually autosuggested by oneself. The best cure is not counter-suggestion but insight into one's autosuggestive process, used to keep challenging negative self-suggestions ([[Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy]], [[Self-Talk]]).[^5]
- Dubois explained neurosis as unintentional, irrational autosuggestion ([[Persuasion Therapy (Dubois)]]).[^6]
- **Evidence.** Supports Ellis's caution. Repeating positive self-statements ("I am a lovable person") improved mood for people with high self-esteem but *worsened* it for those with low self-esteem (Wood, Perunovic & Lee, 2009); cf. [[Ironic Process Theory]].
