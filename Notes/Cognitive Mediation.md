# Definition
> [!info] Cognitive Mediation[^1]
> The principle that emotional responses to events are mediated by cognition (beliefs, judgements, evaluations) rather than caused directly by the events themselves. In [[Epictetus]]'s formulation (*Enchiridion* 5): people are disturbed not by things but by their judgements about things. The event is only the occasion of an emotion; the judgement determines it.

# Properties
- Central to Hellenistic therapeutics, to the Serenity Prayer ([[Dichotomy of Control]]), and to modern [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]]. "The materials of action are indifferent; but the use that we make of them is not indifferent" (*Discourses* 2.5.1).[^1]
- **Hadot.** All Hellenistic schools agreed with [[Socrates]] that evil lies not in things but in the value judgements people bring to them. So people can be cured only by changing their value judgements, and in this sense all of these philosophies aimed to be therapeutic ([[Hellenistic Philosophy]]).[^2]
- **Epictetus's bottle.** The mind is like a bottle of water with light shining through it. When the water is shaken the light looks disturbed, and likewise a disturbed mind projects its disturbance onto external events. The value we attribute to events should be transferred onto the judgements that make them appear that way.[^3]
- **Causal versus constitutive.** Ellis's ABC model ([[Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy]]) treats Beliefs as causal antecedents of emotional Consequences:  + B \to C$. The Stoics go further and treat [[Passion (Stoicism)|passions]] as judgements, so cognition is part of the emotion itself. The constitutive view is probably more consistent with empirical psychology. On either view, changing cognition reliably changes emotion, and if thoughts are constituents of emotions then [[Cognitive Reappraisal|cognitive restructuring]] matters even more.[^4]
	- Clinical consequence: ask "Could you describe your feeling as if it were a thought?" rather than "What thought came just before your feeling?"[^4]
	- "To the extent that we choose what to think, we also choose what to feel" (Stockdale: "The Stoic thinks of emotions as acts of will").[^4]
- **Seneca's model.** Impression, then involuntary [[Propatheia|first movements]], then [[Assent (Stoicism)|assent]], then passion. "The mere appearance is not yet a judgement and not yet an emotion", since emotion is the assent of reason to the appearance. Untrained people assent so automatically that they don't notice assent is a separate act; Stoic training teaches one to stand back and interrogate appearances (Sorabji).[^5]
- People usually experience emotions in stimulus–response language ("A caused C") because C can follow A almost instantly, so the contribution of B goes unnoticed (Ellis).[^5]
- **Interdependence.** Cognition, emotion and behaviour are interacting processes, not separate ones; "one's thinking often becomes one's emotion; and emoting, under some circumstances, becomes one's thought". Emotion may exist without thought "for a moment or two".[^6]
- **Limits.** The principle doesn't exclude non-cognitive causes of emotional disorders. Raw affect from physiology (illness, brain injury, substances) can't be removed by cognitive training, though it can often be moderated by later cognition (Becker); see [[Propatheia]].[^7]
- Seeing that past, habitual and thoughtless decisions now determine what we like or hate gives us the means to revise them consciously.[^8]
- **Evidence.** Appraisal theories of emotion (Lazarus, 1991) are well supported: different appraisals of the same event predict different emotions. In a meta-analysis of experimental studies, instructed reappraisal changed emotional outcomes with a small-to-medium effect (Webb, Miles & Sheeran, 2012).
