# Definition
> [!info] Incentive Sensitization Theory (Robinson and Berridge)[^1]
> Repeated drug use sensitizes the mesolimbic [[Dopamine|dopamine]] system, so drug cues acquire excessive *incentive salience* ("wanting"). Hedonic pleasure ("liking") does not grow and may even decline. Wanting and liking are separable processes (Robinson and Berridge, 1993).

# Properties
- **Nicotine.** Nicotine triggers dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens, telling the brain to pay attention to the situation and repeat the behavior there next time. "Crucially, the smoker doesn't have to feel any pleasure or enjoyment for this to work." (Robert West)[^1]
- It explains how people can compulsively want what they no longer enjoy (Carr: "You haven't enjoyed a single cigarette … that you've ever smoked!").[^2]
- Related: [[Addiction]], [[Craving]], [[Reward Prediction Error Hypothesis]].
- **Wanting vs. liking circuitry.** The wanting system is large: the mesolimbic dopamine pathways from the ventral tegmental area to the nucleus accumbens, plus the dorsal striatum, amygdala and parts of prefrontal cortex. Liking depends on small, scattered "hedonic hotspots", e.g., a hotspot occupying roughly a tenth of the nucleus accumbens shell (Berridge).[^3]
- Dopamine-depleted rats stop seeking food yet still show normal "liking" reactions to sugar (Berridge and Robinson, 1998). Without dopamine, desire dies, and without desire, action stops.[^4]
