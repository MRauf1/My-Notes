# Definition
> [!info] Milgram Obedience Experiment[^1]
> Stanley Milgram's 1960s experiments in which participants, as "teachers", were instructed by an experimenter to give increasingly strong (fake) electric shocks to a "learner" for wrong answers. In the baseline condition, 65% (26 of 40) continued to the maximum 450 V.

# Properties
- **Correction.** Manson says almost none of the participants objected and many seemed to relish their moral righteousness.[^1] In fact most showed visible distress (sweating, trembling, nervous laughter), many protested, and obedience depended strongly on the situation: it fell when the experimenter was absent or when peers refused (Milgram, 1974). Later analyses of the archives found many participants doubted the shocks were real (Perry, 2012).
- **Replication.** A partial replication stopping at 150 V found obedience rates only slightly lower than Milgram's (Burger, 2009).
- **Interpretation.** Obedient participants tended to identify with the scientific goal rather than act from blind obedience ("engaged followership", Haslam and Reicher, 2012).
- Related: [[Asch Conformity Experiment]].
