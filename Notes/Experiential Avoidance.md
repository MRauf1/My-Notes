# Definition
> [!info] Experiential Avoidance[^1]
> (Hayes et al., 1996) Unwillingness to stay in contact with unwanted private experiences (emotions, thoughts, memories, sensations) together with efforts to change their form or frequency, even when doing so causes harm. The opposite stance is *acceptance*: experiencing negative states without trying to eliminate them.

# Properties
- **Meta-emotional amplification.** Because we can have thoughts about thoughts, a negative emotion can trigger a second negative emotion about having it (feeling bad about feeling bad, anxious about being anxious), which feeds itself. Manson calls this the "Feedback Loop from Hell". A culture that treats negative experience as "not okay" strengthens it, and accepting the first emotion breaks the loop.[^1]
- "The avoidance of suffering is a form of suffering. The avoidance of struggle is a struggle. The denial of failure is a failure."[^2] The desire for more positive experience is itself a negative experience, while accepting negative experience is itself positive ([[Backwards Law]]).[^2]
- **Forms.** Denial of problems (delusion and distraction lead to insecurity and emotional repression) and forced constant positivity; both rob one of the feedback needed to solve problems.[^3]
- **Evidence.**
	- Experiential avoidance correlates with anxiety, depression and lower well-being, and predicts worse outcomes over time (Kashdan et al., 2006).
	- Habitually accepting negative emotions and thoughts predicts better psychological health months later, because acceptance lowers the negative emotion felt in response to stressors (Ford, Lam, John and Mauss, 2018).
	- Suppression backfires ([[Ironic Process Theory]]), and acceptance-based therapies such as Acceptance and Commitment Therapy have meta-analytic support (A-Tjak et al., 2015).
- Healthy handling of negative emotions means expressing them in socially acceptable ways that fit one's values, rather than either repressing them or over-identifying with them.[^4]
