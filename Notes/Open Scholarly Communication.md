# Definition
> [!info] Open Scholarly Communication[^1]
> Gray's vision of a world in which all scientific literature and all scientific data are online and interoperate: a reader can go from a paper to its data and redo the analysis, or from data to all the literature about it, increasing the "information velocity" of science.

# Properties
- **Overlay journals**: separate literature and data archives replicated across sites (as PubMed Central made itself portable); a journal is a peer-reviewed "front page" of selected archived articles, whose links raise the articles' search ranking and point back to data; unreviewed (possibly moderated) comments and wikis sit alongside.[^2]
- Archives are cheap to run; the challenges are curation and peer review, and manual curation at scale is prohibitively expensive (about $100 million per year for a million articles), so curation must be automated.[^3]
- **Long-term reproducibility**: how to publish so others can reproduce results in a hundred years? The scientific record, meaning literature plus data plus methods and their links, should allow reproduction from the same data and perturbation of assumptions; yet experimental reproducibility decays as tacit knowledge and technology change over decades.[^4]
- Linking requires semantics: [[Ontology (Computer Science)|ontologies]], schemas, and controlled vocabularies, whose meanings are endlessly debated.[^5]
- Data "mashups" combining datasets require archives for data just as for publications; Gray urged building actual digital libraries for data and literature, not just metadata.[^6]
- Machine-readable literature lets agents read and filter the flood of publications on scholars' behalf, especially across disciplines.[^7]
- International data sharing needs supporting national policy, legal frameworks, and data management practices across jurisdictions.[^8]
- **Openness**: the Internet's disruptive power comes from being public; open source is a second model. Encoding science in open systems lets successors build on it, whereas closed systems must be destroyed to be improved; an open data layer would be a gift to future science. The Web itself became the dominant platform ("Web 2.0").[^9]
- A pillar of [[Data-Intensive Science]]; compare modern literature search in [[AI-Assisted Scientific Research]].
