# Definition
> [!info] Backwards Law[^1]
> (Watts; Huxley's "law of reversed effort") The harder we consciously try to produce a desired mental state, the less we succeed. In particular, the effort to feel happy, and to eliminate the negative (insecurity, uncertainty, failure, sadness), is often what makes us unhappy, anxious and insecure.

# Properties
- **Paradox of hedonism.** "Ask yourself whether you are happy, and you cease to be so" (J. S. Mill). Aiming directly at happiness seems to reduce the chance of attaining it.[^1]
- **Evidence.**
	- People who are induced to value happiness highly feel worse after a happy film, and trait valuing of happiness predicts lower well-being and more depressive symptoms (Mauss et al., 2011; Ford et al., 2014).
	- Trying to feel happy while listening to music lowers the happiness it produces (Schooler, Ariely and Loewenstein, 2003).
	- The cognitive mechanism for mental states is [[Ironic Process Theory|ironic processing]].
- **The "negative path".** Burkeman's alternative: turn towards what we try to avoid by learning to tolerate uncertainty, embrace insecurity, stop forcing positive thoughts, become familiar with failure and value death.[^2] Its sources include [[Stoicism]] ([[Stoic Exercises|negative visualisation]]), [[Buddhism]] ([[Non-Attachment]]) and [[Memento Mori]].
- **Mythological form.** Clinging to one version of a happy life while fighting to remove any chance of an unhappy one causes the problem rather than solving it; struggling to escape your demons is what gives them power.[^3]
- **Psychoanalytic form.** "If you flee it, it'll come back to bite you"; for Freud the unconscious is the store of everything we avoid.[^3]
- **Self-help literature.** Burkeman claims research "strongly suggests" self-help books rarely help.[^4] This overstates the evidence: guided and unguided bibliotherapy based on [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy|CBT]] has meta-analytic support for mild-to-moderate depression (Gualano et al., 2017). The critique applies better to "positive thinking" books than to evidence-based ones.
- **Cult of optimism.** Positive thinking and optimism about the future identify happiness with some other time than now and identify us with our thoughts (compare [[Mind Identification]]). Optimism can be self-confirming either way: success proves it right, failure proves it is needed more.[^5] Compare [[Optimism]] and [[Tragic Optimism]].
- **Overvaluing "doing".** We overvalue positivity and the skills of doing, and undervalue negativity and "not-doing" skills, such as resting in uncertainty or befriending failure, and we seek "closure" too often.[^6]
- Related: [[Happiness]], [[Hedonic Adaptation]], [[Intolerance of Uncertainty]].
