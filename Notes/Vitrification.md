# Definition
> [!info] Vitrification[^1]
> Freezing an aqueous sample so fast (e.g. by plunging it into liquid ethane) that the water solidifies as **vitreous**, i.e. amorphous and non-crystalline, ice. Molecules are fixed in place, hydrated, in whatever orientation and conformation they had at that instant. It produces the [[Cryo-EM Specimen]].

"Cryo" in [[Cryo-Electron Microscopy|cryo-EM]] refers to vitrification, **not crystallization**. Growing an ordered crystal of the protein is the requirement of X-ray crystallography. Cryo-EM needs no crystal, but it does need vitrification.

# Properties
Vitrification is needed for four reasons:
- **Vacuum**: liquid water would evaporate in the microscope column, so freezing keeps molecules hydrated and native.
- **Snapshot**: it locks in random orientations, which supply the views in [[Single-Particle Analysis]], and the conformations, which makes [[Conformational Heterogeneity]] observable.
- **Radiation damage**: low temperature slows beam damage.
- **Vitreous, not crystalline**: crystalline ice would diffract strongly, swamp the weak signal, and its growth would damage the molecules.

The room-temperature alternative, **negative-stain EM** (dried sample embedded in heavy-metal stain), shows only the stain's envelope around the molecule, at roughly 20 Å resolution.

[^1]: [Cryogenic electron microscopy (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryogenic_electron_microscopy)
