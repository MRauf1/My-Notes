# Definition
> [!info] Perspective-Taking[^1]
> Imagining a situation from another person's point of view: their interests, constraints and reasons. Carnegie's Principle 8 is "Try honestly to see things from the other person's point of view." He quotes Henry Ford: "If there is any one secret of success, it lies in the ability to get the other person's point of view and see things from that person's angle as well as from your own."

# Properties
- **Evidence (negotiation).** Perspective-taking, thinking about what the other side *thinks*, helped negotiators find creative deals and gain more. Empathy, feeling what the other side feels, helped less (Galinsky et al., 2008).
- **Limits.** Across 25 experiments, perspective-taking did *not* make people more accurate about what others think or feel. Directly *asking* them, "perspective-getting", did (Eyal, Steffel and Epley, 2018). So pair Carnegie's principle with his other advice: ask questions and listen (see [[Active Listening]]).
- **Talk in terms of their wants.** Carnegie's "bait the hook to suit the fish" and "arouse in the other person an eager want" mean framing a request around the other person's interests.[^2] This is a component of *integrative* negotiation (Fisher and Ury, *Getting to Yes*: focus on interests, not positions).
- **Wants are not only rewards.** Carnegie says "the only way I can get you to do anything is by giving you what you want." This is too narrow. People are also moved by:
	- Intrinsic interest and autonomy ([[Intrinsic Motivation]]).
	- Social norms and identity ([[Pygmalion Effect]]).
	- Reciprocity, consistency ([[Foot-in-the-Door Technique]]) and challenge.
	- Carnegie himself uses several of these in later principles ("appeal to the nobler motives", "throw down a challenge").[^3]
- **Sympathy.** "If I were you I would undoubtedly feel just as you do." Validating feelings defuses conflict.[^4]
- **Attribution.** Assuming there is "a reason why the other man thinks and acts as he does" counters the *fundamental attribution error*, the tendency to blame character rather than circumstance (Ross, 1977).[^5]
- Related: [[Motivated Reasoning]], [[Social Skill]].
