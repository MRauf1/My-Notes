# Definition
> [!info] Pygmalion Effect[^1]
> Higher expectations from others lead to better performance, a self-fulfilling prophecy (Rosenthal and Jacobson, 1968). Carnegie's Principle 7: "Give the other person a fine reputation to live up to." He also says "act as though that particular trait were already one of his or her outstanding characteristics."

# Properties
- **Evidence (smaller than its fame).**
	- Teacher-expectancy effects are real but small on average ($d \approx 0.1$–$0.2$). They are largest when teachers know students least, for example early in the school year (Raudenbush, 1984; Jussim and Harber, 2005).
	- The original study has been criticized for flawed IQ measurement.
	- Leaders' expectations raised subordinate performance in military and work settings, with larger effects in the military (Eden, 1990; McNatt, 2000).
- **Labeling beats persuasion.** In a classroom field experiment, children *told they were* tidy littered less than children *persuaded to be* tidy, and the effect lasted longer (Miller, Brickman and Bolen, 1975). Attributing a trait works partly through self-perception. Carnegie's "appeal to the nobler motives" is a close relative. An example is treating a customer as honest and fair-minded.[^2]
- **Identity framing.** "Don't be a cheater" reduced cheating more than "don't cheat" (Bryan, Adams and Monin, 2013). Replications of the related "be a voter" effect have been mixed, so treat it as tentative.
- **Encouragement.** Carnegie's Principle 8 is to make the fault seem easy to correct. Telling someone they have "an undeveloped flair" supports [[Self-Efficacy]] through verbal persuasion, the weakest of Bandura's four sources. It works best when followed by actual mastery experiences. In Carnegie's flash-card example, each card got right and celebrated was a small success.[^3]
- **The reverse: the Golem effect.** Low expectations lower performance.[^4]
- Related: [[Process Praise]], [[Growth Mindset]], [[Operant Conditioning]].
