# Definition
> [!info] Psychology of Concentration Camp Prisoners[^1]
> ([[Viktor Frankl|Frankl]]) Three phases of the inmate's mental reactions to camp life:
> 1. **Shock** - after admission.
> 2. **Relative apathy** - once entrenched in camp routine, a kind of emotional death.
> 3. **Post-liberation reaction** - depersonalisation, then risks of moral deformity, bitterness, and disillusionment.

# Types
- **Phase 1 (shock)**: *delusion of reprieve* (like the condemned man's illusion at the last minute); striking out one's whole former life; a grim sense of humour ("nothing to lose except our so ridiculously naked lives"); cold, detached curiosity cultivated as protection; near-universal thoughts of suicide, and no fear of death. "An abnormal reaction to an abnormal situation is normal behaviour."[^2]
- **Phase 2 (apathy)**: blunting of disgust, horror, and pity, a necessary protective shell and self-defence mechanism; regression to a primitive level centred on food; absence of sexual urge; "cultural hibernation" except politics and religion; irritability from hunger, lack of sleep, and absence of nicotine and caffeine; an inferiority complex among the degraded majority versus the "promoted" Capos; the imperative "do not be conspicuous"; fear of decisions and letting fate choose.[^3]
- **Phase 3 (liberation)**: "we had literally lost the ability to feel pleased and had to relearn it"; depersonalisation; the body recovers faster than the mind; a compulsive urge to talk; some turned from oppressed to oppressors, justifying it by their experiences, and had to relearn that "no one has the right to do wrong, not even if wrong has been done to them"; bitterness at others' superficiality and disillusionment when no one awaited them.[^4]

# Properties
- Beatings hurt most through the insult and injustice they imply, not the pain.[^5]
- Despite primitivisation, spiritual life could deepen; less hardy prisoners with rich inner lives often survived better than robust ones. Refuges included love for an absent beloved, memories, the beauty of nature and art, humour ("another of the soul's weapons"), and religion.[^6]
- **Relativity of suffering**: like a gas filling any chamber, suffering fills the whole soul whether great or small, so trifles can cause the greatest joy; camp pleasures were a "negative happiness" (freedom from suffering, Schopenhauer).[^7]
- "The best of us did not return": survival often required losing scruples, and luck was decisive; Frankl refused to judge those who favoured friends.[^8]
- There are only two "races" of men, the decent and the indecent, found among guards and prisoners alike; the rift between good and evil runs through every human being.[^9]
- Despite all this, the kind of person a prisoner became was an inner decision: [[Last of the Human Freedoms]], [[Provisional Existence]].
