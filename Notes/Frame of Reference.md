# Definition
> [!info] Frame of Reference[^1]
> A frame of reference (or coordinate system) is the reference relative to which measurements of distance and time are made. Such measurements depend on the observer: two observers moving relative to each other will not in general measure the same distances and time intervals for what appear to be the same events.

# Properties
- Distances and time intervals take different magnitudes depending on the frame of reference used to measure them.
- Per [[Galilean Relativity]], frames of reference moving at constant velocity relative to one another (inertial frames) are physically indistinguishable: the laws of physics take the same form in each.
- Closely analogous to a [[Basis]] combined with an origin in linear algebra: both provide a mathematical scheme for assigning numerical coordinates to an object (a physical position, or an abstract vector) that exists independently of the coordinates used to describe it, and a change between two such schemes (a Galilean or Lorentz transformation, or a change of basis) leaves the underlying object unchanged even though its components change.
- Differs from a basis in three ways: it fixes a physical location as its origin rather than just a set of directions; it must also include a standard for measuring time, not only space; and it can itself be in motion — possibly accelerating — relative to another frame, which (for a non-inertial, accelerating frame) introduces real physical consequences such as fictitious forces that a static change of basis never does.

[^1]: [The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Vol. I, Ch. 5: Time and Distance](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_05.html)
