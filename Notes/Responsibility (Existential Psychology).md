# Definition
> [!info] Responsibility (Existential Psychology)[^1]
> Authorship: in Sartre's sense, being "the uncontested author of an event or a thing". To be aware of responsibility is to be aware of creating one's own self, destiny, life predicament, feelings, and, if such be the case, one's own suffering. It is the clinical face of existential **freedom**, the absence of external structure.

# Properties
- **Constitution of the world**: after Kant, human consciousness provides the form of reality; the world acquires significance only through the "for-itself". The ego is both empirical (an object in the world) and transcendental (constituting itself and the world), which ties responsibility inextricably to freedom. The universe is contingent; one is "doomed to freedom", responsible for one's actions and one's failures to act. Sartre's point is not moral: what one does is one's responsibility.[^2]
- Responsibility awareness is experienced as **groundlessness**, a dizzying *ur-anxiety* (see [[Existential Anxiety]]), and as existential loneliness ([[Existential Isolation]]).[^3]
- **Defenses** against it: seeking structure, authority, and grand designs; compulsivity (an ego-alien "not-me" force); displacing responsibility onto others (paranoia, the therapist); feeling an innocent victim of events one set in motion; being temporarily "out of one's mind", which is purposeful and has payoffs; refusing steps one knows would help; the self-fulfilling prophecy; and, most potently, the appearance of an independent world.[^4]
- Assuming responsibility is a precondition of therapeutic change: if distress is caused externally, the rational strategy is to change the environment, not oneself.[^5]
- **Techniques**: "Whose unconscious is it?"; "I let him bug me" instead of "he bugs me"; changing "can't" into "won't"; "…and I take responsibility for it" (Gestalt); giving a voice to both sides of a conflict; producing or augmenting a symptom; treating dreams as one's own authorship; owning projections (Perls). The most potent method is here-and-now analysis of how the patient recreates their life situation in therapy; the therapist's own feelings are the main instrument.[^6]
- In groups, members should be the primary agents of help; a leader who feels everything depends on them has absorbed the members' responsibility. Leader structure has a curvilinear relation to outcome: too much infantilises, too little bewilders.[^7]
- **Evidence**: avoidance of responsibility is linked to depression via an external [[Locus of Control]] and [[Learned Helplessness]]; patients often cite assuming personal responsibility ("I alone am responsible for my own happiness") as a key therapeutic factor.[^8]
- **Coefficient of adversity**: responsibility does not mean passivity; one should change what can be changed, identify one's true coefficient of adversity, and reconstrue what cannot be altered, since one is always responsible for one's *attitude* toward one's burden (not necessarily one's feelings, but the stand one takes toward them). Compare the Stoic [[Dichotomy of Control]].[^9]
- Responsibility for past creation implies responsibility for future change, and implies [[Existential Guilt]]; one must still locate the boundaries of responsibility and not assume others' ("categorical imperative for responsibility").[^10]
- Turned into action by [[Will (Existential Psychology)|will]] and [[Decision (Existential Psychology)|decision]].
- **Responsibility versus fault (Manson).** Fault is past tense: it results from choices already made. Responsibility is present tense: it results from the choices you are making now. Others may be to blame for your situation, but only you are responsible for how you interpret and respond to it. Blaming gives a temporary high of moral righteousness while handing away the power to solve the problem.[^manson]
- **Chosen versus imposed pain.** The same hardship (e.g. running a marathon) feels empowering when chosen and victimising when forced. Perceived control over a stressor reduces its physiological and psychological harm (Glass and Singer, 1972; Maier and Seligman, 2016).[^manson]
