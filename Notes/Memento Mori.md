# Definition
> [!info] Memento Mori[^1]
> ("Remember you shall die") Rituals, customs and practices designed to encourage regular reflection on one's own mortality, as an everyday habit rather than only when death forces itself on us. Without an ever-present sense of death, "life is insipid. You might as well live on the whites of eggs."

# Properties
- **Finitude increases value.** The more aware you are of life's finitude, the more you cherish it and the less you fritter it away on distractions. Remembering mortality brings you closer to a deathbed perspective, so you live in ways you're less likely to regret.[^2]
- **Nothing to lose.** External expectations and fear of embarrassment or failure fall away before death; "Remembering that you are going to die is the best way I know to avoid the trap of thinking you have something to lose. You are already naked" (Jobs).[^3]
- Facing mortality, the ultimate worst case, can bring an awakening from *how* things are to *that* things are ([[Boundary Situation]]); living more meaningfully lowers death anxiety in a virtuous circle ([[Death Anxiety]]).[^3]
- **Forms.** Medieval *memento mori* art; clock inscriptions such as *Vulnerant omnes, ultima necat* ("every hour wounds, the last one kills"); the Mexican familiarity with death, which "jokes about it, caresses it… celebrates it".[^4] A quick exercise: imagine being eighty and complete "I wish I'd spent more time on…" and "I wish I'd spent less time on…".[^5]
- **Evidence.** Deliberate, reflective contemplation of one's death (as opposed to brief mortality-salience primes) increases gratitude (Frias et al., 2011) and intrinsic, less greedy values (Cozzolino et al., 2004). The eighty-year-old exercise has not been tested directly.
- Prerequisite: reduce the terror of death first, e.g. through the [[Epicurean Argument Against Fearing Death]].[^6]
- Related: [[Stoic Exercises]] (negative visualisation), [[Stoic View of Death]], [[Die Before You Die]], [[Terror Management Theory]], [[Hedonic Adaptation]], [[Mono no Aware]].
- **Stoic form (Robertson).** The meditation on death (*melete thanatou*) is one of the most pervasive themes of ancient contemplative practice and the culmination of [[Premeditatio Malorum]]; Roman generals reputedly had servants whisper *memento mori* during triumphs. It isn't meant to be morbid: the Socratic ideal is to love life yet not fear death. Remembering mortality raises the value of the present ([[Prosoche]]). In the *Phaedo* it is a "separation" (*khorismos*) of soul from body: ceasing to identify with the body and identifying with one's faculty of judgement.[^rob1]
