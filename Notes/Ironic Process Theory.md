# Definition
> [!info] Ironic Process Theory (Wegner)[^1]
> Mental control uses two processes: an effortful *operating process* that searches for contents consistent with the desired state, and an automatic *monitoring process* that searches for contents signalling failure (e.g. the unwanted thought). When effort is excessive or capacity is reduced by "mental load" (fatigue, stress, depression, multitasking), the monitor dominates and produces the opposite of the intended state.

# Properties
- **Thought suppression rebound.** People told not to think of a white bear later think of it more than people who were never told to suppress it (Wegner et al., 1987).
- **Evidence.** A meta-analysis found a small-to-moderate post-suppression rebound but no reliable immediate enhancement during suppression (Abramowitz, Tolin and Street, 2001). The effect is larger under cognitive load (Wegner and Erber, 1992).
- **Ironic effects on emotion and the body.**[^2]
	- Subjects told of a sad event and instructed not to feel sad feel worse than those given no instruction.
	- Panic-disorder patients listening to relaxation tapes show higher heart rates than those listening to neutral audio.
	- Bereaved people who work hardest to avoid grief take longest to recover.
	- People instructed not to think about sex show higher skin-conductance arousal.
- **Positive self-statements.** People low in self-esteem who repeat "I'm a lovable person" feel *worse*, as the message conflicts with their self-concept and they reassert their existing self-image against it; high self-esteem people benefit slightly (Wood, Perunovic and Lee, 2009).[^3] See [[Self-Talk]] and [[Self-Esteem]].
- The remedy suggested by acceptance-based therapies is to allow the thought or feeling rather than suppress it (see [[Non-Attachment]]).
- Mechanism behind the [[Backwards Law]]; see also [[Rumination]].
