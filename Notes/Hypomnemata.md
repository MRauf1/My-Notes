# Definition
> [!info] Hypomnemata[^1]
> Personal philosophical notebooks (Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations* is the surviving example) used to formulate, condense and repeatedly reread philosophical maxims so that they become internalised and are available under stress. They were not "cathartic writing" for venting feelings. As Antisthenes told a student who had lost his notebook: "You ought to have written them on your mind and not on paper."

# Properties
- **Reformulation.** Marcus repeatedly restates basic Stoic principles in different words, condensing as much rhetorical power into as few words as possible to engrave them in memory. Epictetus says such notes were meant to be reread repeatedly.[^2]
- They also use the opposite strategy: stripping away emotive language ([[Objective Representation (Stoicism)]]).[^2]
- **Self-monitoring.** Epictetus noted that "diseases of the soul" strengthen with repetition (a practice effect, as in [[Habit]] formation). He had students record how often they lapsed into, e.g., rage and track their progress. This anticipates CBT self-monitoring records, which often produce insight, objectivity and distance on their own (Beck, Emery & Greenberg; [[Cognitive Distancing]]).[^3]
- **Why memorisation matters.** Therapeutic philosophy depends on fully internalising key rules of living and recalling them in stressful situations. Writing can discourage memorisation, which is why oral teaching and mnemonic strategies were used. Insight that is forgotten is of little benefit, and under stress people revert to errors like overgeneralisation ([[Cognitive Distortion]]).[^4]
- **Dogmata.** At the top of the Stoic hierarchy of *dogmata* was a handful of core principles, simple to state but needing lifelong study to assimilate. Classical maxims such as *gnothi seauton* ("know thyself"), *meden agan* ("nothing in excess") and [[Memento Mori|memento mori]] were designed to be instantly memorable.[^5] Spinoza: frame fixed practical precepts and commit them to memory so they are "always ready to our hand".[^6]
- **Evidence.** Thought records are a standard CBT component. Self-monitoring alone often changes the monitored behaviour (*reactivity*), usually in the desired direction (Korotitsch & Nelson-Gray, 1999).
- One of the [[Stoic Exercises]]; cf. [[Philosophy as a Way of Life]].
