# Definition
> [!info] Research Heuristics[^1]
> Practical rules of thumb for learning and pursuing research problems, as collected by Manuel Blum: read and write actively, learn from others' solutions, seek contradictions and disproofs, and persist with what you love.

# Types
- **Read with random access** - books are random access, unlike scrolls; start anywhere rather than feeling obliged to read front to back.[^1]
- **Write what you read** - writing as you read is one of the most enjoyable and profitable ways to learn hard material ([[Writing]]).[^1]
- **Ask "How should I have been thinking?"** - when someone else solves a problem you failed on, or solves it better, study their solution to learn how you should have thought.[^2]
- **Read the prior publication** - when you solve a problem and find it already published, read it: roughly 50% of the time it is substantially different, 25% the same but worse, and 25% better, so at least half the time you can still publish, and otherwise you can learn.[^2]
- **Exploit contradictions** - if you can prove both $S$ and $\neg S$, something is wrong somewhere; contradiction is one of the most potent sources of knowledge ([[Contradiction]], [[Proof by Contradiction]]).[^3]
- **Try to disprove** - while proving $S$, spend some time trying to prove it false; it gives insight even if $S$ is true, and intuition is too often wrong. The best "search" disproves what one most believed; the answer to a yes/no question may be neither.[^4]
- **Keep a list of problem-solving strategies** - write down good ways to pursue a problem ([[Heuristic|heuristics]]).[^5]
- **Look for nonstandard answers** to hard enough problems, and don't conclude a problem is unsolvable just because an expert couldn't solve it: read, talk to others, and keep working.[^6]
- **Treat rejection as feedback** - use reviewers' comments, however irritating, to improve the paper and resubmit.[^7]

Lessons from Guo's *The Ph.D. Grind*:[^9]
- **Outputs trump inputs** - research credit comes from producing outputs, not consuming classes or papers; undirected reading binges in a vacuum yield little, whereas goal-directed literature search (finding competitors, adapting good ideas) is effective. Ideas beget ideas: substantive ideas rarely arise in a vacuum, so find a solid starting point.
- **Understand collaborators' incentives** - collaborators "hungry to publish" (mid-stage students, untenured faculty) behave very differently from those who are not; learn others' motivations and research tastes to "lead from below" by proposing ideas that excite them.
- **Create lucky opportunities** - repeatedly put yourself and your work on display (talks, conversations, offering and asking for help, gratitude); most efforts yield nothing, but serendipity requires trying. Endorsement by an influential person matters, since good work alone is not enough to be noticed.
- **Give many talks** - early talks yield design feedback, pre-submission talks reveal common criticisms, and all improve speaking and handling hostile questions.
- **Sell, sell, sell** - acceptance often hinges on how well a paper's pitch appeals to reviewers' tastes; alignment with the subjective preferences of senior colleagues and reviewers matters even in "objective" fields, and skilled framing (e.g. honestly extracting insight from a failed evaluation) can rescue a paper.
- **Ally with insiders** - insiders know the tricks for publishing in their subfield; working as an outsider (e.g. straddling several subfields) is enriching but brings more rejections.
- **Reject bad defaults and know when to quit** - defaults naturally favour those in power, e.g. funded "default" projects that count as successful regardless of how many students fail on them; judge whether your project is promising and quit gracefully if not. Failure is inevitable, so learn to recover.
- **Set short-term deadlines** - self-imposed deadlines prevent ruts and chronic procrastination.
- **Grind hard and smart** - creative ideas mean nothing without thousands of hours of unglamorous, consistent effort, but effort on the wrong angle, tools, or errands is wasted; grinding smart requires perceptiveness, intuition, and asking for help.
- **Help generously** - peers are not in zero-sum competition, so exchange feedback on ideas and drafts before external review.

Lessons from Hamming's *You and Your Research*:[^10]
- **Work on [[Important Problem|important problems]]**, and keep [[Tolerance of Ambiguity|tolerance of ambiguity]].
- **Courage and confidence** - believe you can do important problems, or you almost surely won't; one success breeds confidence. Great scientists usually had independent thoughts young and the courage to pursue them; great work takes more than brains.
- **Drive compounds** - knowledge and productivity are like compound interest: of two people of similar ability, one who works 10% more will more than double the other's output. Deep commitment beats dabbling talent; you must neglect other things; but misapplied drive gets nowhere, so apply effort sensibly. Deep immersion lets the subconscious work on the problem.
- **Keep planting acorns** - early fame makes it hard to work on small problems, which can sterilise great scientists who try to get the big thing right off.
- **Work with the door open** - closed doors yield more work now, but open doors yield clues to what is important; those with open doors more often do important things.
- **Turn faults into assets** - by a change of viewpoint, apparent defects (including bad working conditions) become assets; know your weaknesses and manage yourself, e.g. committing publicly to force yourself to perform. Be honest with yourself.
- **Sell the work** - write clearly, give formal and informal talks, and in talks give the broad picture and why it matters before details; devote around 50% of effort to presentation.
- **Work with the system** - fighting the system, ego assertion, and anger each cost a steady price over a career; use and adapt the system instead, and prefer amusement to anger. Originality in science tends to come with some originality elsewhere, but keep its cost low.
- **Read for problems, not solutions** - reading too much makes you think as others did; clarify a problem and think it through before looking at answers. Papers stimulate in the short term, but books that digest a field and keep only the essence matter long-term.
- **Shift fields every ~7 years** - originality in a field gets used up, so move to something nearby for new viewpoints, even at the cost of reputation.
- **Choose collaborators well** - talk to capable people rather than holding brainstorming sessions, avoiding "sound absorbers"; seek first-class people who expect great work from you.
- **Research vs. management** - pursue what you can do single-handedly; move toward management only when your vision exceeds that, and be clear which path you chose.
- The value is in the struggle more than the result; great science brings stress, and fame is a dividend ([[Productive Struggle]]).

# Properties
- "Brains are muscles": they grow strong with exercise and weak without it (cf. [[Growth Mindset]], [[Productive Struggle]]).[^5]
- You must like your work enough to keep thinking about it long after everyone else has moved on.[^8]
- Compare problem choice via the [[Feasibility-Interest Diagram]] and advice in [[You and Your Research (Hamming)]].
