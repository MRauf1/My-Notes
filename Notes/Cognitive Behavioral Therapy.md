# Definition

Combination of [[Behavior Therapy]] and [[Cognitive Therapy]].[^1]

Helps clients examine how their thoughts affect their [[Behavior]]. It helps clients to change how they think and how they act.
# Stoic Roots (Robertson)
- **Definition (Dobson & Dozois).** CBT rests on three assumptions: (1) cognitive activity affects behaviour; (2) cognitive activity can be monitored and altered; (3) desired behaviour change can be brought about through cognitive change. Several therapies fall within this "broad church"; the two most influential are Ellis's [[Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy]] and Beck's [[Cognitive Therapy]].[^rob1]
- **Not rationalisation.** CBT aims to help clients deal with irrational or disturbing emotions and cultivate rational, proportionate ones instead. It is arguably anti-rationalist in its emphasis on behavioural experiments: beliefs are tested in "the laboratory of our personal experience".[^rob2]
- **Stoic precursor.** Both Beck and Ellis cited Stoicism as a philosophical precursor. Of all the ancient schools, Socratic philosophy and especially Stoicism most resemble CBT. Both make cognition central to the cause and cure of emotional disturbance ([[Cognitive Mediation]]), and both stress responsibility, rationality and disciplined self-observation.[^rob3] The main difference is scope: ancient philosophy also offers a way of life and a cosmic perspective ([[Philosophy as a Way of Life]], [[View from Above]]).[^rob4]
- **Two analogies.** Stoic literature describes philosophy through the medical model (philosophy as therapy) and the skills-training model (philosophy as athletics, *askesis*), both of which carry over to psychotherapy. Ellis: dispute irrational beliefs "over and over, until you build intellectual and emotional muscle".[^rob5]
- Stoic mindfulness ([[Prosoche]]) is the aspect of Stoicism that early CBT neglected most.[^rob6]
- **Stoic parallels to CBT techniques:** [[Cognitive Distancing]], [[Premeditatio Malorum]] (imaginal exposure), [[Contemplation of the Sage]] (modelling), [[Prospective and Retrospective Meditation]] (problem solving), [[Hypomnemata]] (self-monitoring), [[Philosophical Empathy]], [[Cognitive Distortion]]. Historical bridge: [[Persuasion Therapy (Dubois)]].
- **Consequences.** Both traditions pause to consider the consequences of different actions or attitudes; Skinner: reasons for behaving "are almost always consequences". The ancient symbol was the Pythagorean fork: the easy path of error (*kakia*) or the effortful path of excellence (*arete*) leading to [[Eudaimonia]]. To change someone's philosophy of life, show them that their current beliefs are not in their own best interest.[^rob7]
