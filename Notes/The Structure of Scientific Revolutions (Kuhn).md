# Review[^1]

Kuhn is a very eloquent writer, and I'd like to to learn to emulate his prose.

Historically, there are always various theories for understanding different phenomena, and the road to research consensus has always been arduous and full of obstacles.

Early on, due to the absence of a unified paradigm, the research often times is seemingly random compared to the later developments, and focus on the easily accessible experiments and data.

Even for similar phenomena experienced, the initial researchers oftentimes interpret these phenomena differently, leading to divergences in thinking, but even these divergences eventually largely disappear when a scientific consensus is reached due to a triumph of one of these paradigms.

To be a paradigm, a theory must be better than its competitors, but it does not have to fully explain all of the facts of the phenomena.

As time goes on, whichever theory can attract the new generation's practitioners is the one that gains prominence while its competitors slowly wither out with its remaining practitioners left to obscurity in time.

In mature scientific fields, books either act as pedagogical books or as reflections on the past. They do not represent the border of human knowledge at which research happens, like they do during pre-paradigm eras. In mature scientific fields, research happens in the form of research papers. This also means that laymen cannot simply understand and start SOTA research just be reading books anymore - books have become the initial step towards research, not the final.

Initially, a paradigm is very immature and presents itself as an object for further articulation and specification under more stringent/new conditions. This is where most of the exciting work and research is to be done. This process oftentimes does not take into account phenomena that don't fit into this framework and can be intolerant of competing theories. Instead, this type of scientific work focuses on progressing the theories and phenomena that are articulated by the paradigm itself. This kind of detailed focus allows for very detailed and esoteric work to be done in this particular field, which would usually be unimaginable under pre-paradigm circumstances. So even if this paradigm eventually ceases to work effectively, and another paradigm takes over, that detailed work that was done would not have been for nothing and may still apply. This period/framework under a specific paradigm is what Kuhn calls "normal science".

In my case, the battle between LLMs and World Models can be seen as the pre-paradigm era of current deep learning.

For most normal science research problems and experiments, they do not aim to produce major novelties. Their hypothesis are generally what they expect, and they aim to push the field one step forward. These projects do not generally aim at unexpected novelty. Even so, these scientists still treat these incremental projects as significant for the understanding and articulation of the paradigm. However, even more than this, what makes research significant and fascinating is not the outcome itself, which oftentimes can be expected in advance and may not seem like all that much, but the process with which one arrives at the outcome because this said process is oftentimes in doubt and unclear. It can be seen as puzzle-solving, which is inherently satisfying for the scientists to solve and prove themselves.

Paradigms generally provide a suite of research questions that which can be solved within that paradigm. Part of the reason why science moves rapidly once a paradigm is established is because the main barrier preceding discovery is the scientist's ingenuity. Questions not articulated by the paradigm are left alone and oftentimes ignored.

While many can be excited by the prospect of research in the sense that it advances human understanding to a new territory or is a way to prove one's own usefulness or some other general reason, this is not the case for the individual researcher. For the individual, their main affixation is on the puzzle-solving that demonstrates that this individual has solved this puzzle that no one has ever done before or solved this puzzle in a new way. And the researcher can achieve this only if they are skillful enough in the puzzle-solving. Thus, for an individual, the motivation and the excitement of research comes from a primarily individual reason. And it is this type of puzzle-solving process that has addicted many a scientist.

These puzzles are described not only by assured solution(s), but also by rules that limit both the nature of acceptable solutions and the steps with which they are reached.

Unexpected discoveries start about as anomalies that violate the paradigm-induced expectations. This leads to an adjustment of the paradigm theory such that the anomaly becomes expected within the paradigm itself. Until then, this anomaly is not considered a scientific fact. This unexpected discovery is not found deliberately because one expects it. A large portion of these are discovered by accident. Part of them are discovered because there is some kind of inconsistency within the paradigm that the researcher is trying to solve. These kinds of unexpected discoveries may not easily fit into the usual style of "X discovered this concept at time Y". Sometimes, it takes a while for these kinds of discoveries to trigger a change in the paradigm. These anomalies come with a resistance that is set against the background of expectation. Even though normal science tries to ignore these anomalies and focus on the expected discoveries, this rigidness to the expected is exactly what ends up giving rise to the anomalies that arise against the common expectations of the researchers.

Paradigm procedures and applications are just as important as paradigm laws and theories and inconsistencies in these too can lead to paradigm changes.

Insecurity results from the paradigm's inability to explain certain phenomena, which leads to the invention of new theories and laws, which leads to paradigm shift/change.

Even though externalities are indifferents, how you use them and what you make of them reflects on whether you are acting in accord with nature. As such, don't treat externalitites as important to you, but do not treat them carelessly either.

In the presence of anomalies, researchers do not necessarily renounce the existing paradigm. There is no research without the paradigm as rejecting a paradigm without substituting another one is akin to rejecting science altogether. Instead, they try to come up with ad hoc modifications to the paradigm to "account" for these anomalies. And when a new paradigm comes forth, not only are the two competing paradigms compared with nature, but they are also compared with each other, and only then, can the new paradigm replace the old paradigm.

In the presence of a crisis, the rules of puzzle-solving loosen and the paradigm itself blurs as to allow the emergence of a new paradigm. In this sense, research during the crisis is similar to research during the pre-paradigm period, except that the locus of focus is narrower and smaller. During this process, the paradigm is reconstructed from the new fundamentals, and though there may be overlap between which problems the 2 different paradigms can solve during the transition process, the overlap is not complete until the end at which point the new paradigm completely subsumes the older one. The new paradigm oftentimes starts to emerge before the crisis has developed far or has been explicitly recognized. Othertimes, considerable time passes between the beginning of the crisis and the emergence of a new paradigm. In the latter case, a scientist may experiment randomly without a concrete theory in an attempt to understand the anomaly. In cases like these, it is not uncommon for scientists to leverage the philosophical analysis as a device to unriddle their fields. In such a way, a crisis often proliferates new discoveries.

Interestingly, it is most often people who are either young or new to the field (and hence in a way detached from the current paradigm) who achieve fundamental inventions of a new paradigm. This transition to a new paradigm is hence a scientific revolution.

Certain paradigms are still valid and can never be challenged as long as they stick to their own core assumptions that have been experimentally verified. For example, Newtonian dynamics works as a very good approximation of Einsteinian dynamics when the velocity is low relative to the velocity of light. It's only when one tries to use Newtonian dynamics outside of its scope by applying it to near light speed phenomena that it breaks down, but of course it does, when Newtonian dynamics has only been verified for low velocity phenomena. Seen in this case, Newtonian dynamics can be thought of as a special case of Einsteinian dynamics (not exactly though).

A scientific revolution can change the way scientists look and interact with the world in new ways. The different perspectives are also a function of the paradigm with which the scientists work.

People learn about science and such through textbooks and such, but those do not talk about revolutions. Instead, they talk about the outcomes of revolutions and the current accepted paradigms; they do not discuss the beginning, progression, and conclusion of the scientific revolution, how the paradigms were challenged, how a new paradigm was created and battled against the existing paradigm, what discoveries challenged people's understanding, which is why scientific revolutions oftentimes feel invisible to most people. Even when a new one pops up and concludes, the textbooks are rewritten as to incorporate the outcomes of this new revolution without discussing its progression and details. As a result, unless one has personally lived through and experienced the revolution progressing, scientific revolutions of the past oftentimes are invisible to most modern people.
