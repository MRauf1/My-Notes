# Definition
> [!info] Process Praise[^1]
> Praise directed at the process by which a person achieved something (effort, strategies, choices, persistence, seeking input), tied to the resulting learning or progress, as opposed to **ability (person) praise** that judges intelligence or talent. Ability praise induces a [[Fixed Mindset|fixed mindset]]; process praise fosters a [[Growth Mindset|growth mindset]].

# Properties
- **Dweck's praise study**: after ability praise, students rejected challenging tasks they could learn from, while 90% of effort-praised students chose them. After a set of hard problems, ability-praised students concluded they were not smart after all, lost enjoyment, and their performance plummeted even on easy problems, ending worse than they started; effort-praised students used the hard problems to sharpen their skills and improved. Almost 40% of ability-praised students lied about their scores, always upward. Telling children they are smart made them feel dumber, act dumber, but claim to be smarter.[^2]
- Intelligence praise gives only a momentary glow: at the first snag, "if success means they're smart, then failure means they're dumb."[^3]
- Praise may be enthusiastic; only praise that judges traits, or implies pride in talent rather than work, is to be avoided. Accomplishments can be appreciated and then tied to the process; showing interest in and asking about the process often suffices without praise.[^4]
- For children with learning disabilities, often it is not sheer effort but finding the right strategy that works.[^5]
- **Pitfalls**: praising effort that is not there, and using effort praise as a consolation prize when no learning occurs; ineffective effort should prompt new strategies and resources, not contentment.[^6]
- Praising speed and perfection (the enemies of difficult learning) sends a fixed-mindset message; when a task is too easy, withhold praise and offer something one can learn from.[^7]
- Reassuring children about their intelligence or talent backfires, making them more afraid of showing a deficiency; fixed judgments of *others* in front of children also communicate a fixed mindset ("Am I next?").[^8]
- **Constructive criticism** helps fix something, not labels or excuses; protecting children from failure and withholding honest feedback harms their future.[^9]
- Workplaces with a "praised generation" needing constant reassurance can instead praise initiative, seeing difficult tasks through, learning from struggle, resilience to setbacks, and openness to criticism.[^10]
- A special case of [[Mindset Transmission]].

- **Carnegie's version.** "Praise the slightest improvement and praise every improvement," and make praise *specific* so it comes across as sincere.[^11] Praise motivates when it is sincere and specific, attributes success to controllable causes such as effort and strategy, supports autonomy, and conveys attainable standards. Generic or controlling praise can undermine motivation (Henderlong and Lepper, 2002). See also [[Feedback Intervention Theory]] and [[Pygmalion Effect]].
