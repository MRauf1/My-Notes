# Definition
> [!info] Child Machine[^1]
> Turing's proposal that, instead of programming a machine to simulate the adult mind directly, one should program a machine that simulates a child's mind and then subject it to an appropriate course of education, thereby obtaining an adult mind.

# Properties
- Decomposes the problem of building an adult mind into (i) the initial state of the mind (the child programme) and (ii) its education, i.e. an early statement of [[Machine Learning|learning]] as the route to [[Artificial Intelligence]], anticipating the [[Bitter Lesson]] preference for meta-methods over built-in knowledge.
- Can be taught by punishments and rewards: the machine is constructed so that events shortly preceding a punishment signal become unlikely to be repeated, while a reward signal increases the probability of repeating the events that led to it. This is the principle of [[Operant Conditioning]] and the [[Law of Reward]], and an early form of [[Reinforcement Learning]]; the definitions presuppose no feelings on the part of the machine.[^2]
- Rewards and punishments alone are a weak teaching channel: if the teacher has no other means of communication, the information reaching the pupil cannot exceed the total number of [[Reward (Artificial Intelligence)|rewards]] and punishments applied.[^3]
- Hence "unemotional" channels of communication are also needed: using rewards and punishments, the machine can be taught to obey orders given in a symbolic language transmitted through these channels, which greatly reduces the number of rewards and punishments required.[^4]
- In the 1948 formulation, one begins with a machine with very little capacity for elaborate operations or disciplined reactions to orders (given as interference), then applies appropriate interference mimicking education until it reliably produces definite reactions to commands. The machine is an [[Unorganized Machine|unorganized machine]], trained e.g. as a [[Pleasure-Pain System]].[^5]
- Generalised by Bostrom's [[Seed AI]], which can improve its own architecture rather than merely accumulate content.
- Turing's constructive answer to [[Lady Lovelace's Objection]], since an educated machine does more than it was explicitly told; the idea first appears in [[Intelligent Machinery (Turing)]].
