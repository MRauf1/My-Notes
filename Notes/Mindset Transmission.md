# Definition
> [!info] Mindset Transmission[^1]
> The process by which parents, teachers, and coaches pass on a [[Mindset|mindset]] to children and students: not through the beliefs in their heads but through their deeds, namely (1) how they praise, (2) how they respond to setbacks, and (3) whether they focus on deepening understanding.

# Properties
- **Praise**: parents' praise molds children's mindsets independently of the parents' own mindset (see [[Process Praise]]).[^2]
- **Setbacks**: reacting to a child's failure with anxiety, concern about ability, or even glossing over it conveys that failure is an issue and fosters a fixed mindset; treating setbacks with interest as a platform for learning, and discussing next steps, transmits a growth mindset.[^3]
- **Understanding**: teachers who teach for deep understanding, with support, move students toward a growth mindset; treating a subject (e.g. math) as rules and procedures to memorise does not, even if effort is emphasised. High-stakes-test memorisation may promote fixed mindsets and ironically undermine test performance.[^4]
- Children are sensitive to judgment messages from toddlerhood, and we never outgrow the sensitivity: fixed-mindset children feel their traits are measured constantly and that love and respect depend on fulfilling parents' aspirations, while growth-mindset children feel helped. Abused children learned early that distressed peers are to be judged and punished.[^5]
- **Discipline**: "Don't judge. Teach." Judging and punishing teaches only that rule-breaking is punished, not how to reason to mature ethical decisions, and closes communication; growth-minded parents set high standards, teach how to reach them, and say a fair, respectful no.[^6]
- **Ideals**: fixed-mindset ideals (the brilliant, talented child) cannot be worked toward and leave no room for error or individuality; growth-minded ideals give something to strive for and room to grow.[^7]
- **Great teachers** believe in the growth of intellect and talent and are fascinated by learning. They combine high standards for *all* students, a nurturing atmosphere of trust rather than judgment, and teaching *how* to reach the standards; lowering standards produces entitled, poorly educated students, while raising them without means pushes students out. They continue to learn along with their students.[^8]
- Fixed-mindset teachers judge beginning performance, decide who is smart, and give up on the rest; students then sabotage the teacher by not trying ([[Self-Handicapping]]).[^9]
- **Coaches** (e.g. John Wooden): ask for full preparation and effort rather than mistake-free games ("Did I make my best effort?"), give to all players equally, train fundamentals, and help players become a little better every day. Success can knock a team into a fixed mindset.[^10]
