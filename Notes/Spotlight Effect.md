# Definition
> [!info] Spotlight Effect[^1]
> The tendency to overestimate how much others notice and judge our appearance and actions (Gilovich, Medvec and Savitsky, 2000). Carnegie: "people are not thinking about you and me or caring what is said about us. They are thinking about themselves."

# Properties
- **Evidence.** Students made to wear an embarrassing T-shirt estimated that about 50% of peers noticed it. In fact about 25% did (Gilovich, Medvec and Savitsky, 2000). The bias arises from anchoring on our own vivid experience of ourselves and adjusting insufficiently for others' perspective.
- **Related biases.**
	- *Illusion of transparency*: we overestimate how visible our internal states are to others (Gilovich, Savitsky and Medvec, 1998).
	- People also overestimate how harshly others will judge their public blunders (Savitsky, Epley and Gilovich, 2001).
- **Criticism.** Carnegie's advice on criticism has three parts:
	- Do your best and let unjust criticism "drain off" you.
	- Treat unjust criticism as often a "disguised compliment".
	- Do *not* ignore just criticism.
	- Knowing the spotlight effect makes the first two easier.[^2]
- **Seeking critique.** Keep a record of your own mistakes and actively ask for "unbiased, helpful, constructive criticism".[^3] Compare [[After-Action Review]].
- Related: [[Social Anxiety Disorder]], [[Cognitive Bias]], [[Self-Compassion]].

- **Pausch's version.** "If nobody ever worried about what was in other people's heads, we'd all be 33 percent more effective." The 33% figure is invented. As a manager he told his team he would always say what he thought, so silence meant nothing was wrong, which removed the need to guess.[^4]
