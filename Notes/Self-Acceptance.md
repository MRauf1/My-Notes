# Definition
> [!info] Self-Acceptance (Adlerian Psychology)[^1]
> Accepting one's irreplaceable "this me" as it is, including one's "incapable self", and having the courage to change what can be changed. One cannot discard or replace the "I", but one can change how one uses it. It is the first of the three ingredients of [[Community Feeling]].

# Properties
- **Self-acceptance vs self-affirmation.** Self-affirmation tells oneself "I can do it" even when something is beyond one's ability. It can lead to a [[Superiority Complex]] and is a form of lying to oneself. Self-acceptance accepts that one cannot do something and moves forward doing what one can.[^2]
- **What can and cannot be changed.** One cannot change what one is born with, but one can change what use one makes of it. Focus on what can be changed. This mirrors the Serenity Prayer: "God grant me the serenity to accept the things I cannot change; courage to change the things I can; and wisdom to know the difference." Cf. [[Dichotomy of Control]].[^3]
- **Affirmative resignation.** Resignation in the sense of seeing things clearly with fortitude and acceptance. It is not pessimistic.[^4]
- **Courage to be normal.** Wanting to be special comes from not accepting one's normal self. Being normal is not being incapable. With the courage to be normal, the world looks very different.[^5]
- Corresponds to the objectives "to be self-reliant" and "the consciousness that I have the ability".[^6]
- Related: [[Self-Compassion]], [[Self-Esteem]].
- **Normal is individuality.** Being normal is an aspect of individuality and nothing to be ashamed of. One's place exists without being special. Feeling insulted by "normal" shows one still wants to be a "special me" and is still living within problem behavior ([[Five Stages of Problem Behavior]]). Place worth on being yourself, not on being different. Individuality is absolute, not relative.[^kk1]
- The Serenity Prayer again: accept what cannot be changed, change what can.[^kk2]

# Evidence
- Positive self-statements ("I am a lovable person") made people with low self-esteem feel *worse* (Wood, Perunovic & Lee, 2009), which supports the warning against self-affirmation in this sense. Note that "self-affirmation" in research usually means affirming one's core values (Steele, 1988), and that practice reliably reduces defensiveness (Cohen & Sherman, 2014). It is a different concept.
