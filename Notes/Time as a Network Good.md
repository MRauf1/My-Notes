# Definition
> [!info] Time as a Network Good[^1]
> Time is not only a regular good, more valuable the more of it you control, but also a *network good*: its value depends on how many other people have access to it and how well their time is coordinated with yours. Having all the time in the world is of little use, or even unpleasant, if you must spend it alone.

# Properties
- **Evidence.**
	- In Sweden, the more people were on holiday at the same time, the better the population's well-being, including fewer antidepressant prescriptions, even among retirees (Hartig et al., 2013).[^2]
	- Unemployed people get a happiness boost at weekends, partly from shared free time and partly from relief from the shame of not working (Young and Lim, 2014).[^3]
	- Acting in synchrony increases cooperation (Wiltermuth and Heath, 2009). A meta-analysis found a small-to-moderate effect of synchrony on prosocial behavior (Mogan, Fischer and Bulbulia, 2017).[^4]
- **The Soviet continuous week** staggered workers' rest days so factories never stopped. Its main effect was to destroy social life, and it was abandoned by 1940.[^5]
- **The trade-off.** Every gain in personal time sovereignty costs ease of coordination. More freedom over when you work makes it harder to be free when your friends are.[^6] Atomized, isolated individuals form the base of totalitarian movements (Arendt).[^7]
- **Time affluence still matters.** Spending money to buy time, for example by outsourcing chores, raised life satisfaction in several studies (Whillans et al., 2017). The evidence suggests both control over time and synchrony with others contribute to well-being.
