# Definition
> [!info] Gratitude Intervention[^1]
> A deliberate practice of noticing and recording what one is grateful for, such as listing blessings weekly or writing gratitude letters. Carnegie's Rule 4: "Count your blessings, not your troubles!"

# Properties
- **Evidence.**
	- People who listed blessings weekly reported higher well-being than those listing hassles (Emmons and McCullough, 2003).
	- Meta-analyses find *small* effects. Gains over neutral control activities are modest ($d \approx 0.1$–$0.3$) (Davis et al., 2016; Dickens, 2017).
	- Effects on anxiety and depression are small, $d \approx 0.3$ (Cregg and Cheavens, 2021).
	- Gratitude letters show the most reliable short-term gains (Seligman et al., 2005).
- **Negativity bias.** "We seldom think of what we have but always of what we lack."[^2] Negative events and information weigh more heavily than positive ones (Baumeister et al., 2001). Gratitude deliberately counters this, as does [[Cognitive Reappraisal]].
- **Downward comparison.** Carnegie's "I had no shoes… I met a man who had no feet" is *downward social comparison*. It can lift mood temporarily (Wills, 1981) but tends to backfire when the comparison is threatening or self-relevant.[^3]
- **Dosage.** Counting blessings once a week was more effective than three times a week, possibly because the practice goes stale (Lyubomirsky, Sheldon and Schkade, 2005).
- **Expecting gratitude.** Carnegie's Rule 3 is to expect ingratitude and give for the joy of giving. See [[Prosocial Behavior]].[^4]
- Related: [[Happiness]], [[Stress]].

- **Expressing gratitude to others (Pausch).** A handwritten thank-you note persuaded Pausch to admit a graduate applicant.[^5] People underestimate how positively recipients react to gratitude letters and overestimate how awkward they will feel, so they send fewer than they should (Kumar and Epley, 2018).
