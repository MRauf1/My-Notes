# Definition
> [!info] Easyway Method (Allen Carr)[^1]
> A method for quitting that doesn't resist the desire with willpower but removes the desire. It dismantles the belief that the drug provides pleasure or a crutch. Once the drug is seen to do nothing for you, quitting involves no sacrifice and so needs no willpower. "You need willpower only when you have a conflict of wills."

# Properties
- **Evidence.** In a randomized controlled trial, a single Easyway seminar did as well as England's specialist stop-smoking service with medication (Frings et al., 2020). Biochemically verified abstinence at 26 weeks was 19.4% vs. 14.1%. That trial tested the in-person seminar, not the book alone.
- **Against the willpower method (Carr).** Quitting by willpower creates a feeling of deprivation. Deprivation makes you miserable, which increases the desire, so the struggle never ends. Failing then reinforces the belief that quitting is impossible.[^2] This fits the finding that people with high self-control experience fewer temptations, rather than winning more fights against them (Hofmann et al., 2012; see [[Willpower]]).
- **Quit outright; don't cut down.** Cutting down makes each dose seem more precious and increases the illusion of pleasure.[^3] *Supported*: abrupt quitting gave higher 6-month abstinence than gradual reduction (22.0% vs. 15.5%; Lindson-Hawley et al., 2016).
- **No "one cigarette".** "Think of a hundred thousand cigarettes." Keeping open the option to start again is a big mistake.[^4] *Supported*: any smoking in the first two weeks strongly predicts relapse (Kenford et al., 1994). The "not a puff" rule is standard in stop-smoking support.
- **No doubt.** A smoker under stress has two decisions to make: the real problem, and whether to smoke. "When you have a choice … until you make that decision, you will be distracted by the choice." Removing the choice removes the pressure of deciding.[^5]
- **Quit for yourself.** Quitting for someone else's sake breeds a sense of sacrifice.[^6] *Supported*: autonomous motivation predicts long-term abstinence (Williams et al., 2006; see [[Intrinsic Motivation]]).
- **Untested instructions.** These include no substitutes, no lifestyle changes, no need to avoid triggers, a final-cigarette ritual, and starting with elation rather than gloom. Standard cessation advice still recommends avoiding triggers early on.[^7]
- **Contradicted claims.**
	- *Scare tactics backfire.* Fear appeals are effective on average (Tannenbaum et al., 2015), and pictorial warnings increase quit attempts (Brewer et al., 2016).
	- *Nicotine replacement keeps people trapped.* Nicotine replacement raises quit rates by about 50–60% (Cochrane, Hartmann-Boyce et al., 2018), and nicotine e-cigarettes do better still (Cochrane, Lindson et al., 2024).
	- *Withdrawal is purely mental.* See [[Nicotine Withdrawal]].[^8]
