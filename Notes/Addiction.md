# Definition
> [!info] Addiction[^1]
> Compulsive use of a substance (or behavior) despite harm, maintained by a learned *wanting* for it rather than by genuine pleasure. In Carr's words, it "has nothing to do with 'liking' the drug, 'enjoying' the drug or getting 'pleasure' or 'benefits' from the drug."

# Properties
- **Little Monster and Big Monster (Carr).** The *Little Monster* is the mild physical withdrawal, an "empty, insecure feeling", that the drug itself creates. The *Big Monster* is the belief that the drug gives you pleasure or a crutch. It develops because you seem to feel better after each dose, and it interprets the Little Monster as "I want a cigarette".[^2]
- **Vicious circle.** Each dose only partially relieves the discomfort caused by the previous one, so each dose creates the desire for the next. "Nicotine doesn't fill a void; it creates one." The misery of addiction is caused, not relieved, by the drug ([[Deprivation Reversal Model]]).[^3]
- **Wanting without liking.** This matches [[Incentive Sensitization Theory]]: nicotine's dopamine release links smoking to the situations where it happens, and "the smoker doesn't have to feel any pleasure or enjoyment for this to work" (Robert West).[^4]
- **Cues.** Conditioned cues trigger craving, e.g., smokers like the smell of smoke because they associate it with relief of craving (see [[Cue]], [[Craving]]).[^5]
- **Habit vs. addiction.** Carr insists addiction is not a [[Habit]]: a habit is repeated for the comfort of familiarity, while a smoker smokes to relieve withdrawal. Research partly disagrees: compulsive drug use is often modeled as a shift from goal-directed to habitual, cue-driven control (Everitt and Robbins, 2005), though how much habit explains addiction is still debated.[^6]
- **Mind vs. body.** Carr claims addiction is "1 per cent physical and 99 per cent mental". The split is rhetorical and overstated, but expectation does shape craving strongly (see [[Nicotine Withdrawal]]).[^7]
- Accepting that you are addicted, rather than "in the habit" or enjoying it, is the first step of Carr's method ([[Easyway Method]]). See also [[Addictive Personality]].[^8]
- **Context.** Addiction is strongly tied to context: of soldiers addicted to heroin in Vietnam, only about 5% relapsed within a year of returning home (Robins, 1974; see [[Habit Discontinuity Hypothesis]]).[^9]
