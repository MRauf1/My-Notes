# Definition
> [!info] Reward Prediction Error Hypothesis[^1]
> The hypothesis that phasic activity of midbrain [[Dopamine|dopamine]] neurons encodes not reward itself but the *temporal-difference error*: a fluctuation in the animal's expectation (its value function) indicating that an earlier prediction was in error. "Temporal-difference learning didn't just resemble the function of dopamine. It was the function of dopamine."

# Properties
- History: Olds and Milner found rats would press a lever stimulating the septal area up to 5,000 times an hour for 24 hours; the most compelling sites involved dopamine neurons, which are few but broadcast widely ("they cannot say much to the rest of the brain, but what they say must be widely heard"). Yet dopamine-blocked rats failed to *learn* lever-food associations, and dopamine responses did not track reward, attention, novelty, or surprise per se, so dopamine could not simply represent reward, until [[Temporal-Difference Learning|TD learning]] resolved the riddle.[^2]
- Reinforcement learning has left a permanent mark on the neuroscience of decision-making (Niv).[^3]
- **Implications for happiness**: artificially elevated dopamine produces an inevitable crash, since things cannot keep seeming better than expected; we can chemically fool the prediction mechanism, but not forever. If happiness comes from outcomes exceeding expectations, lasting pleasant surprise is unsustainable as expectations adapt.[^4]
- Lowering expectations is no simple fix, since positive expectations themselves raise happiness before outcomes arrive (Rutledge).[^5]
- "Agents that know the world perfectly have zero expected happiness": complete mastery correlates with boredom, with ethical implications for RL agents and humans.[^5]
- Novel and surprising events also trigger dopamine regardless of reward, linking it to [[Curiosity-Driven Exploration]].[^6]
