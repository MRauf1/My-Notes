# Definition
> [!info] Electric Field[^1]
> The vector field $\mathbf{E}$ produced by a charge $q$ at displacement $\mathbf{r}$ from it,
> $$
> \begin{align}
> \mathbf{E} = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\mathbf{r}}{r^3}
> \end{align}
> $$
> such that a second charge $q'$ placed at that location experiences a [[Force]] $\mathbf{F} = q'\mathbf{E}$, per [[Coulomb's Law]].

# Properties
- Separates the analysis of an electric interaction into two independent parts — what produces the field, and how a charge responds to the field at its location — a division that becomes indispensable, rather than a mere bookkeeping convenience, once interactions are not instantaneous (see [[Electromagnetic Field Momentum]]).
- Exists at a point in space whether or not a charge is actually there to respond to it, and "remembers" the past positions of its sources even after they have moved; this is why the field itself is treated as a physically real entity, not just a restatement of the force law.
- The total field due to multiple sources obeys the [[Superposition Principle]]: it is the vector sum of the fields each source would produce alone.
- A moving charge in an electric and magnetic field experiences the full [[Lorentz Force Law]], not just $q\mathbf{E}$.
- Recoverable as the negative gradient of the scalar [[Electric Potential]] $\phi$: $\mathbf{E}=-\nabla\phi$, mirroring $\mathbf{F}=-\nabla U$ for a general [[Conservative Force]].

[^1]: [The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Vol. I, Ch. 12: Characteristics of Force](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_12.html)
