# Definition
> [!info] Feasibility-Interest Diagram[^1]
> (Alon) A tool for choosing a research problem by placing candidates on two axes: *feasibility*, how hard the problem is (e.g. expected time to completion), which depends on the researchers' skills and the lab's technology; and *interest*, the expected increase in verifiable knowledge, i.e. how far the problem ventures from "the known shores". One usually seeks problems in the top-right quadrant: feasible and of high interest.

# Properties
- Problems that are easy on paper are often hard in reality, and those hard on paper are nearly impossible in reality.[^1]
- A common mistake is taking the first problem that comes to mind; since projects take years even when they seem doable in months, time spent choosing (e.g. not committing for 3 months) saves months or years later.[^2]
- **Inner voice**: ranking problems with one's inner voice yields problems that satisfy in the long term. Aids: asking "If I was the only person on earth, which of these problems would I work on?"; noticing ideas that recur for months or years; and noticing how it feels to describe each project. Caring mentors and a supportive environment strengthen this voice.[^3]
- The inner voice expresses a personal filter on the world tied to one's values (what is good, beautiful, and true), which is what each scientist uniquely contributes; a multiplicity of such styles underlies a creative science. Choosing well thus requires reflecting on one's world view.[^4]
- Self-expression makes work revitalising, self-driven, and meaningful, and more likely to discover something profound; the more you interest yourself, the more likely you interest your audience.[^5]
- Compare Hamming's [[Important Problem|important problems]], defined by having a reasonable attack ([[You and Your Research (Hamming)]]) and the prioritisation of urgent, elastic problems under [[Crucial Consideration]].
- The research process that follows is described by the [[Nurturing Schema of Research]].
