# Definition
> [!info] Mathematical Writing[^1]
> The communication of mathematics in written form, an important part of doing mathematics itself: besides informing the reader, it is an exercise in clarifying and structuring one's own thinking, and it has an art and elegance every writer should strive for.

# Types
Guidelines (Su):
- *Know your audience* - pitch the level of detail to the intended reader.[^1]
- *Set an invitational tone* - invite readers into the reasoning with the present tense, "we" rather than "I", and gentle commands ("let $n$ be...", "recall that...", "consider the set of...").[^1]
- *Use complete sentences* - equations are part of sentences and take punctuation: a period when the thought is complete, a comma when it continues.[^2]
- *Avoid shorthand in formal writing*.[^2]
- *Learn the etiquette* - avoid beginning a sentence or phrase with a number or symbol, and emphasise (italicise or underline) unfamiliar terms at the point where they are defined.[^2]
- *Decide what's important to say* - present just enough detail and highlight the most interesting or unexpected parts of the argument; the primary role is to give insight.[^3]
- *Highlight structure* - for long technical arguments, outline the argument at the start and signpost progress through it; use paragraphs for related blocks of ideas, with crucial first sentences.[^4]
- *Choose good examples* - and avoid [[Red Herring|red herrings]].[^4]
- *Step back and simplify*.[^4]
- *Refine, refine, refine* - revision is also what clarifies one's own thinking.[^4]
- *Observe the culture* - good communication is inseparable from the culture in which it takes place.[^5]

Guidelines (Halmos):[^6]
- *Say something* - the basic problem is to communicate an idea: have something to say, someone to say it to, organise it, arrange it, write and rewrite it, and work hard on diction, notation, and punctuation. Having something to say is by far the most important ingredient: a sufficiently important idea can make even a confusing work immortal, but relying on this alone is rarely possible and never desirable. As with a sequence having no limit (no cluster points or too many), writing fails to have a subject through no ideas or too many.[^7]
- *Speak to someone* - specify the audience both broadly and as a particular person (e.g. a deliberately obtuse, friendly colleague); the audience determines motivation, informality, detail, and repetition. A definite aim that hits something else beats an aim too vague to hit anything.[^8]
- *Organise first* - the expositor's main contribution is organising and arranging material to minimise the reader's resistance and maximise insight. *Organisation* (headings and their connections, a graph, likely a tree, not a chain) matters more than *arrangement* (its linear order), which still has psychological value; good organisation is tight, with unobtrusive wires visible only when the structure is tampered with. Organisation continues throughout writing and after.
- *What to leave out* - too much detail is as discouraging as none. Mathematics is concrete examples and problems; general theories are afterthoughts built on insights from special cases, so organise around the crucial examples and counterexamples, and guide the reader toward what a proof does *not* prove.
- *Design notation at the start* - plan alphabets for every kind of object in advance; ad hoc mid-sentence notation is almost certainly bad, and bad notation makes good exposition bad. The best notation is no notation: pretend you are explaining on a walk in the woods with no paper.
- *Write steadily* - write a little every day without exception, and prime the pump for the next session (plan tomorrow's section and a short title for it, as a test that the section has a unified subject; end on an up-beat). Draft freely, then rewrite rather than edit ([[Spiral Plan (Writing)]]).
- *Honesty* - tell the reader the status of every statement (proved, not proved, to be proved, won't be). "Obvious" is fine if it was still obvious months later, was accepted by others by demonstration rather than intimidation, and above all is true. Do not drag in irrelevant assumptions or misplace emphasis. Complete honesty makes for greatest clarity.[^9]
- *State theorems cleanly* - motivation first, then the statement, then the proof; ideally one short sentence without chit-chat. Long lists of hypotheses and conclusions are a badly expounded theory, whose hypotheses likely describe a concept worth isolating and naming.[^10]
- *Repetition* - repeat phrases word for word to highlight a small change, "with a proper roll of drums"; but never repeat a proof: similar steps signal an unfound lemma from which both results follow more easily.
- *Unobtrusive style* - clarity not pedantry, understanding not fuss; correct spelling avoids distraction. Halmos prefers the neutral voice (no first-person pronouns, and never "one"), with simple declarative sentences and the imperative ("To find $p$, multiply $q$ by $r$"), unlike Su's invitational "we".[^11]
- *Words and symbols* - avoid technical terms, especially new ones; name necessary new ones carefully; use old ones correctly and consistently. Consistency is a cardinal virtue in language, notation, references, and typography.
- *Punctuation* - punctuate symbolic sentences like verbal ones; don't overwork periods or commas; never start a sentence with a symbol (use apposition: "The set $X$..."); and use "then" with "if".
- *Save the reader's time* - eight hours of work that saves five minutes of each reader's time saves over 80 person-hours per 1000 readers.

Guidelines (Knuth, Larrabee, and Roberts):[^12]
- *Don't use symbols in titles* - titles (of papers, sections, theorems) should be readable in words alone, which is also useful for indexing and search.

# Properties
- Giving insight rather than a bare chain of steps is what gives an argument a favourable [[Smell (Mathematics)|smell]] and makes a [[Proof|proof]] understandable.
- Cf. [[Structured Proof]] for a formal alternative to prose proofs.
- An instance of [[Writing]] as a tool for thinking; compare [[How to Write a Great Research Paper (Jones)]] and [[Math Writing (Stanford University)]].
